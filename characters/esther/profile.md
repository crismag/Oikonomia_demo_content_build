## Who Esther is

Esther, whose Hebrew name was Hadassah, was a Jewish orphan in the Persian citadel of Susa. Her cousin Mordecai raised her as his own daughter after her father and mother died.

When King Ahasuerus deposed Queen Vashti, his officials had young women gathered from across the empire into the royal harem under the custody of the king's eunuch Hegai. Esther "was taken" with them. Scripture does not record her consent, her feelings or any choice she had. After twelve months of beauty treatments, each young woman was taken to the king for one night and then moved to the house of the concubines, unless the king called for her by name. When Esther's turn came, she asked for nothing beyond what Hegai advised. The king made her queen. On Mordecai's instruction she did not reveal that she was Jewish.

Some years later the king's highest official, Haman, enraged that Mordecai would not bow to him, obtained a royal decree to destroy all Jews in the empire on one day. Mordecai urged Esther to go to the king and plead for her people. She explained that anyone who approached the king uninvited could be put to death, and that she had not been called for thirty days. Mordecai answered that she would not escape because she lived in the palace, and asked, "Who knows if you haven't come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"

Esther asked all the Jews in Susa to fast for her for three days, and said she and her maids would fast too. "Then I will go in to the king, which is against the law; and if I perish, I perish." She went in. The king extended his sceptre. Instead of making her request at once, she invited the king and Haman to a banquet, and then to a second one. At the second banquet she revealed that she and her people had been sold to be destroyed, and named Haman. Haman was executed. Esther then went back to the king, weeping at his feet, because Haman's decree still stood. A counter-decree, written as Mordecai directed, allowed the Jews to defend themselves. On the appointed day they killed their attackers throughout the empire, including five hundred men and Haman's ten sons in Susa. At Esther's request the king granted a second day of fighting in Susa and the public hanging of the sons' bodies. Esther and Mordecai wrote to establish the festival of Purim.

God is not named anywhere in the book of Esther.

In 2026 Esther is a senior leader on the Leadership Council whose work centres on advocacy and sensitive leadership. She is the leader people want in the room when someone vulnerable needs a voice, when confidential information must be handled carefully, and when a request to someone powerful has to be made well.

## 2026 voice

- Measured and deliberate. She speaks less than most people in the room and chooses her moment.
- Courteous and precise when asking for something: context first, request exactly worded, conditions clear.
- Plain about risk. She says what it could cost and does not dramatise it.
- Asks for support before acting ("fast for me") and names the people carrying it with her.
- Emotion is honest and restrained: "How can I endure to see the destruction of my relatives?" is her register, not outbursts.
- Keeps confidences. She discloses when disclosure will protect people, and not before.
- Contemporary English, no King James imitation, and no palace-drama tone.

She should not sound like Deborah (direct command and prophetic confidence), Mordecai (watchful, persistent, pushing), Priscilla (teaching and home fellowship) or Miriam (worship leadership). Esther is the advocate who prepares, waits, risks and speaks.

## Strengths

- Courage that grows under pressure rather than being assumed from the start.
- Willingness to risk her own safety for people who could not reach the king.
- Asks for communal support and prayerful preparation before acting (the three-day fast).
- Strategic patience: she did not rush her request, and the text shows the delay proving significant.
- Precise, well-framed petitions that name the harm and the person responsible.
- Persistence after an apparent victory: she returned to the king because the decree was still in force.
- Gives authority to others: Esther set Mordecai over Haman's house.
- Formalises remembrance: her own letter confirmed Purim.

## Development areas

- **Hesitation under fear.** Her first response to Mordecai was to explain why she could not go (4:11). This is not a flaw to mock; the danger was real. It is the starting point of her courage.
- **Silence and concealment.** She concealed her identity on Mordecai's instruction (2:10, 20). Whether that concealment was wise, necessary or compromising is a matter of interpretation; the text does not judge it. In 2026 it becomes development material about when confidentiality protects and when silence only protects oneself.
- **The second day in Susa.** Esther asked for another day of killing in Susa and for Haman's sons' bodies to be hanged (9:13). The text does not state her reasons or evaluate the request. Data Play must not celebrate it as triumph or turn it into a simplistic condemnation. It is grave material about power, fear, justice and vengeance.
- **Using access.** Esther worked through proximity to an unpredictable king. Leading through access rather than formal authority carries its own limits.

## Humor

Esther is not a comic character. Very light material is available where nothing grave is involved:

- her carefully conditional phrasing ("If it pleases the Council…") appearing in an ordinary request, noticed in comments;
- her refusal to be hurried: she has a request, and she will make it tomorrow, at the time she has chosen;
- colleagues trying to guess from a short, deliberate Events guest list what Esther is about to raise.

Humor must never come from the harem, her being taken, beauty treatments or the king's bed, Vashti's deposition, the threat of genocide, the fast, Haman's execution, Esther 9 or the hanging of Haman's sons. No romance framing and no pageant comedy.

## Sensitivity

- **Coercion and sexual exploitation.** Esther was taken into a royal harem under a system that gathered young women for the king's use (2:2–4, 8, 12–14). The text uses the language of favour and love from the king's side (2:17); it does not record Esther's consent. Never romance, never a "beauty contest" or "Cinderella" framing.
- **Vashti** refused to be displayed before drunken officials and was deposed by decree (1:10–22). Treat her with dignity.
- **Genocide.** Haman's decree ordered the killing of all Jews, "both young and old, little children and women" (3:13). Never comedy.
- **Esther 9.** Tens of thousands were killed, including 500 men and Haman's ten sons in Susa, 300 more on the second day, and 75,000 across the provinces. The Jews did not take plunder. This violence is grave and its moral assessment is debated. Present it plainly; do not glorify or sanitise it.
- **Haman's execution** (7:7–10) is not a punchline.
- **Identity and threat** should not be casually mapped onto contemporary political conflicts.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| Hadassah, that is, Esther; daughter of Abihail, Mordecai's uncle; raised by Mordecai as his daughter | Biblical fact (Esther 2:7, 15) |
| "Hadassah" means myrtle; "Esther" is a Persian or Babylonian name (sometimes linked to "star" or to the goddess Ishtar) | Common interpretations of the names, not stated in the text |
| Her age, her feelings on being taken, and whether she consented | Not recorded. Do not invent. |
| Ahasuerus is Xerxes I (reigned about 486–465 BC) | Common identification, not stated in Scripture. Qualify it; do not present as certain. |
| Esther ate non-kosher food or otherwise compromised her faith in the palace | Speculation; the text does not say. Do not use. |
| Mordecai married Esther | Later rabbinic tradition; not Scripture. Do not use. |
| "Relief and deliverance will come to the Jews from another place" (4:14) refers to God | Widely held interpretation; the text does not name God. Some read it as other human help. |
| The fast involved prayer | Reasonable inference; prayer is not mentioned in the text. |
| Why Esther delayed her request to a second banquet | Not stated. The narrative shows the delay proving significant (chapter 6). |
| Why Esther requested a second day in Susa and the hanging of Haman's sons' bodies | Not stated. The ten sons had already been killed (9:7–10); the hanging was public display. |
| The counter-decree permitted attacking the enemies' "little ones and women" (8:11) | The wording mirrors Haman's decree. Whether it authorised attacks on the attackers' families or refers to the Jews' own families is debated. The account of the fighting names men (9:6, 12, 15). |
| God is not named in the book | Fact for the Hebrew text used by WEB. |
| The Greek Additions to Esther (prayers of Esther and Mordecai, and others) | Found in the Septuagint and some Christian canons, not in the Hebrew text. Data Play uses the Hebrew book. |
| Historicity of the book | Debated by scholars. Data Play treats it as Esther's lived history without arguing the question. |
