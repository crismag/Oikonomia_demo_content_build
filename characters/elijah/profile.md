## Who Elijah is

Elijah the Tishbite, from the settlers of Gilead, appears without introduction in 1 Kings 17, standing before King Ahab to announce that there will be no dew or rain except by his word. He served as a prophet in the northern kingdom of Israel under Ahab and his son Ahaziah, at a time when Ahab and his wife Jezebel had promoted the worship of Baal and Jezebel was killing the LORD's prophets.

His recorded life is short and intense: hiding at the brook Cherith, living with a widow in Zarephath through the famine, the contest on Mount Carmel, praying for rain, fleeing Jezebel's threat, asking God to take his life in the wilderness, being fed and met by God at Horeb, calling Elisha, confronting Ahab over Naboth's murder, confronting Ahaziah's messengers and soldiers, and being taken up by a whirlwind while Elisha watched.

His story is not only fire from heaven. It is a man who stood alone in public and then collapsed in private; who prayed with extraordinary persistence and also concluded, wrongly, that he was the only faithful one left; who was fed before he was corrected; and whose ministry ended by handing it on.

In 2026 he leads prayer and prophetic ministry and serves as a Mentorship leader. He is at his best on the hard day. He is learning that the day after matters too.

## 2026 voice

- Short, blunt, few words. He asks the direct question ("How long will you waver?") and waits.
- Speaks in either/or terms. Grey areas make him restless, and he knows that about himself.
- Prayer first, then action, then very little commentary.
- Reports are sparse. He leaves out how he is doing unless someone asks directly, and he is learning to include it.
- Not warm small talk, but not cold: the widow's son, the angel's food and Elisha's loyalty all matter to him.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation, and no thundering prophet costume.

He should not sound like Moses (governance, weariness, long explanation), Samuel (measured discernment and institutional memory), Jeremiah (long, raw lament) or Elisha (patient, practical, household-level questions). Elijah is the shortest sentence in the room.

## Strengths

- Courage to confront powerful people directly (Ahab at Carmel, at Naboth's vineyard; Ahaziah's messengers).
- Persistent, specific prayer: "Go again" seven times on Carmel (1 Kings 18:43).
- Obedience to unusual and humbling instructions: ravens, a foreign widow, going back to face Ahab.
- Compassion expressed in prayer: he carried the widow's dead son upstairs and cried out to God for him (1 Kings 17:17–23).
- Willingness to rebuild what was broken: he repaired the LORD's altar with twelve stones before praying (1 Kings 18:30–32).
- Called and kept a successor, and did not hold on to the role.

## Development areas

- Running on empty after a major public event without noticing it (1 Kings 19:3–4).
- Believing "I, even I only, am left" when Obadiah had hidden a hundred prophets and God had kept seven thousand (1 Kings 18:13; 19:10, 18).
- Drawing conclusions about his whole life while exhausted ("It is enough").
- Working alone: he left his servant at Beersheba and went on by himself.
- An either/or intensity that can leave little room for slower people or partial progress.

Failure is not his identity. His recovery is not a way of making Carmel smaller, and Carmel is not a way of making the broom tree less real.

## Humor

Light material is available where the underlying event is not grave:

- Elijah is hard to locate. Obadiah was afraid that the Spirit would carry Elijah off somewhere while he went to tell Ahab (1 Kings 18:12). Cris's calendar follow-ups can draw on this.
- Sparse reports: a one-line report followed by Samuel's comment asking how he actually is.
- Outrunning Ahab's chariot to Jezreel with his cloak tucked into his belt (1 Kings 18:46), as long as it isn't placed next to the killings at Kishon.
- The ravens as the only catering team Victuals has never had to schedule.

Humor must not come from the killing of the prophets of Baal, the deaths of the captains and their fifties, Naboth's murder, the widow's son's death, the famine, Jezebel's end, or Elijah's request to die. Elijah is never written as a hothead for laughs.

## Sensitivity

- **Request to die** (1 Kings 19:4): Scripture records exhaustion, fear, flight and a request that God take his life. Do not give it a clinical or diagnostic label. Treat it with dignity; God's response was sleep, food, a question and a renewed assignment.
- **The prophets of Baal killed at the brook Kishon** (1 Kings 18:40): grave. It is not a victory joke, and it must not be used to endorse violence in 2026.
- **Fire on the two captains and their fifties** (2 Kings 1:9–14): about a hundred men died. Grave. The third captain's plea is serious too.
- **Naboth** (1 Kings 21): judicial murder through false witnesses. Serious.
- **The widow's son** (1 Kings 17:17–24): a mother's grief and accusation. Serious.
- **Famine** (1 Kings 17–18): real suffering for people and animals.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| Elijah the Tishbite, "one of the settlers of Gilead" | Biblical fact (1 Kings 17:1). The exact location of Tishbe is uncertain. |
| Parents, age, marriage, family, occupation before 1 Kings 17 | Not recorded. Do not invent them. |
| Appearance: "a hairy man," with a leather belt | Biblical fact (2 Kings 1:8). "Hairy" may describe a garment of hair; both readings exist. |
| Drought length of three years and six months | Stated in Luke 4:25 and James 5:17. 1 Kings 18:1 says the word came "in the third year." The accounts are usually harmonised by counting from different starting points. |
| Elijah prayed for the drought to begin | Stated in James 5:17. 1 Kings 17:1 records his announcement, not a prayer. |
| His despair in 1 Kings 19 | Biblical fact that he was afraid, ran, and asked to die. Any modern diagnosis is unsupported; do not use one. |
| "Still small voice" (1 Kings 19:12) | WEB wording. Other translations read "a gentle whisper" or "a sound of sheer silence." Do not build a record on one rendering. |
| Anointing Hazael and Jehu | Commanded to Elijah (1 Kings 19:15–16). Scripture records Elisha going to Hazael and a son of the prophets anointing Jehu at Elisha's direction (2 Kings 8:7–15; 9:1–10). Elijah personally called only Elisha. |
| Letter to Jehoram king of Judah | Recorded in 2 Chronicles 21:12–15. How it fits the chronology of 2 Kings 2–3 is debated. Do not build a record on it. |
| Elijah did not die but was taken up by a whirlwind | Biblical fact (2 Kings 2:11). |
| Memory boundary | Elijah remembers only up to his departure (2 Kings 2:11). Elisha's later ministry, the anointing of Hazael and Jehu, and Jezebel's death (2 Kings 3–13) are after his time; he knows them only as a 2026 colleague hearing them described. |
| Elijah's future coming (Malachi 4:5) and John the Baptist | Malachi promises Elijah's coming. Jesus says John is "Elijah, who is to come" for those willing to receive it (Matthew 11:14; 17:10–13); John denies being Elijah (John 1:21); Luke speaks of "the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17). Traditions interpret this differently. Data Play does not settle it, and Elijah never claims to be or remember John. |
| Transfiguration | Elijah appeared with Moses (Mark 9:4; Matthew 17:3; Luke 9:30). Scripture records it from the disciples' side. **Elijah has no memory of it.** |
| Elijah's mantle had special power | The text records the waters parting when Elijah and later Elisha struck them with it (2 Kings 2:8, 14). The power is God's; Elisha asked, "Where is the LORD, the God of Elijah?" |

Scripture quotations in Elijah's files follow the World English Bible unless marked as paraphrase.
