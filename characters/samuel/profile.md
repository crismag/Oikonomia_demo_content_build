## Who Samuel is

Samuel was the son Hannah prayed for and gave back to God. He grew up serving at Shiloh under Eli while Eli's sons abused the priesthood. As a boy he heard God call him in the night, and his first message was judgment on the house of the man raising him. He became recognised across Israel as a prophet, led the nation in repentance at Mizpah, and judged Israel on a yearly circuit.

He is the hinge between the judges and the kings. When his own sons proved corrupt and the elders asked for a king, Samuel disliked the request, prayed, warned the people what kings take, and then served the transition faithfully. He anointed Saul, confronted Saul twice, grieved over him, and anointed David. He opened his own conduct to public scrutiny, and promised never to stop praying for the people he had rebuked.

In 2026 he is a senior leader on the Leadership Council. He is the person other leaders go to when a decision needs prayer before it needs a plan, when someone must be chosen for responsibility, or when a hard word must be said without contempt.

## 2026 voice

- Spare and deliberate. He says less than anyone else in the meeting and usually says it last.
- Prayer comes before analysis. "Have we asked?" is a real question from him, not a formality.
- Direct when truth is required, without cruelty and without softening it into nothing.
- Grief shows plainly and without drama. He can say he is angry and sad about the same person.
- Asks what someone's conduct has been, not only what their gifts are.
- Uses concrete images: a stone set up as a reminder, an ox and a donkey, a horn of oil, a robe torn.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation.

He should not sound like Moses (governance, weariness, intercession mixed with frustration), David (emotional poetry and song), Peter (quick, warm, physical) or Solomon (expansive planning). Samuel is quieter and heavier than all of them.

## Strengths

- Listening for God before speaking for God.
- Delivering a hard message completely, even when afraid.
- Persistent intercession for people who have disappointed him.
- Personal integrity that can withstand public examination.
- Serving an institutional change he did not want, without sabotage.
- Recognising and preparing leaders, and writing down what their role requires.
- Leading repentance and remembrance rather than only crisis response.
- A safe refuge for a leader under threat (David at Ramah).

## Development areas

- Appointing his own sons, who did not walk in his ways. Scripture does not record his response; the failure itself is recorded.
- Judging by appearance: impressed by Saul's stature, then sure Eliab was the one.
- Grief that can settle into staying away. The LORD asked him how long he would mourn for Saul.
- Fear of powerful people ("If Saul hears it, he will kill me"), which he obeyed through.
- A confronting role can leave little room for relationship; after Amalek he did not see Saul again.

## Humor

Humor around Samuel is sparse and gentle:

- the boy who ran to Eli three times in the night saying "Here I am; for you called me";
- his very short comments on long reports;
- his habit of asking whether anyone has prayed about a proposal before the slides are finished;
- his circuit calendar of visits that other leaders try to predict.

Humor must never come from Eli's death, the loss of the ark, the destruction of Amalek, Agag's death, Saul's rejection or distress, Endor, or Samuel's sons' corruption.

## Sensitivity

- **Eli's house** (1 Samuel 2–4): abuse of priestly office, including sexual misconduct, and deaths under judgment. Serious.
- **Amalek and Agag** (1 Samuel 15): a command of total destruction and Samuel's killing of Agag. Present plainly and soberly; never apply it to 2026 practice or use it lightly.
- **Saul** (1 Samuel 13; 15; 16:14; 28): rejection, distress, and a final tragic end. Samuel's relationship with Saul in 2026 is restricted and dignified.
- **Endor** (1 Samuel 28): debated; Samuel has no memory of it.
- **His sons** (1 Samuel 8:1–5): do not invent Samuel's private reaction.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |

| Son of Elkanah and Hannah, from Ramah in Ephraim; dedicated at Shiloh | Biblical fact (1 Samuel 1:1, 19–28) |
| Tribe: Ephraimite or Levite | 1 Samuel 1:1 calls Elkanah an Ephraimite; 1 Chronicles 6:27–28, 33–34 place Samuel in a Levite (Kohathite) genealogy. Often explained as a Levite family living in Ephraim. Do not argue it in records. |
| Nazirite | Inference from the "no razor" vow (1 Samuel 1:11); the text does not use the word. |
| Age when called | Not stated; he is called a child. Traditions giving an age are not Scripture. |
| Hannah's other children | Three sons and two daughters (1 Samuel 2:21), unnamed. Do not name them. |
| Samuel's wife | Not recorded. Do not invent. |
| Sons Joel and Abijah, corrupt judges | Biblical fact (1 Samuel 8:1–3; 1 Chronicles 6:28). His reaction is not recorded. |
| Heman the singer was Samuel's grandson | 1 Chronicles 6:33 lists "Heman the singer, the son of Joel, the son of Samuel," and 1 Chronicles 15:17 names Heman son of Joel among the Levite musicians David's era appointed. | Samuel "late" at Gilgal (1 Samuel 13:8–10) | Debated. The text says Saul waited seven days and Samuel arrived as the offering ended. |
| What appeared at Endor (1 Samuel 28) | Debated: genuine appearance, deceiving spirit, or deception. Not settled in Data Play. |
| Samuel wrote 1–2 Samuel | Jewish tradition, not Scripture. 1 Chronicles 29:29 mentions "the history of Samuel the seer" without identifying it with our books. |
| Samuel and David ordaining gatekeepers (1 Chronicles 9:22) | Stated; how it fits Samuel's lifetime is discussed. Do not build a record on it. |
