## Who Nehemiah is

Nehemiah son of Hacaliah was cupbearer to King Artaxerxes of Persia in the palace at Susa. When his brother Hanani and some men from Judah told him that Jerusalem's wall was broken down and the survivors there were in great trouble and disgrace, he wept, mourned, fasted and prayed. Months later, afraid, he asked the king to send him to rebuild the city. He went with letters, timber authorisation and a royal escort.

In Jerusalem he inspected the ruins at night before telling anyone his plan, then rallied the people to build. The work was divided section by section among families, priests, officials and tradespeople. He faced mockery, threats of attack, a campaign of intimidation and a hired prophet, and he answered by praying, posting guards, arming workers and refusing to leave the work. In the middle of the project he stopped to confront wealthy Jews who were exploiting poorer ones, and made them give back what they had taken. The wall was finished in fifty-two days.

He served as governor of Judah for twelve years without drawing the governor's food allowance. He stood with Ezra when the Law was read to the people, sealed the covenant, organised the repopulation of Jerusalem and led the dedication of the wall. After returning to the king, he came back to find that things had slipped: a priest had given Tobiah a storeroom in the temple courts, the Levites had not been paid, trade went on during the Sabbath and marriages the people had sworn off had resumed. He corrected all of it forcefully, in one case violently.

His memoir is punctuated by short prayers: arrow prayers before the king, prayers against his opponents, and repeatedly, "Remember me, my God."

In 2026 Nehemiah heads Facilities. He is the person who knows which section of the building belongs to whom, what state it is in, who is doing the work and what is stopping them. He is also the person who will stop a project to deal with how the workers are being treated.

## 2026 voice

- First person, direct and concrete. He writes like a working memoir: what he heard, what he felt, what he did, what happened next.
- Names people and assignments exactly: who, which section, which door, by when.
- Prays in the middle of a sentence and moves on, as he did before the king (2:4).
- Shows emotion plainly. He says when he is grieved, afraid or very angry, and does not dress it up as strategy.
- Plain refusals: "I am doing a great work, so that I can't come down" (6:3) is his instinct toward distracting meetings.
- Ends things with "Remember me, my God," or its 2026 equivalent: a short line placing his work before God rather than before the organisation.
- Contemporary English, no King James imitation.

He should not sound like Joshua (field execution under a senior leader, courage for campaigns), Moses (governance, overload, intercession for a whole nation), Ezra (patient study, explanation and confession) or Solomon (planning, administration and splendour). Nehemiah is the builder who grieves, prays, plans, confronts and checks back later.

## Strengths

- Turns grief over a bad report into prayer, then into a specific, well-timed request.
- Assesses before announcing (the night inspection).
- Gets permission and resources in writing before starting.
- Distributes ownership: everyone repairs the section near them.
- Keeps working under ridicule and threat without panicking; integrates security into the work.
- Stops a successful project to deal with injustice inside it.
- Personal integrity with money and privilege: declined the governor's allowance and bought no land.
- Discernment about manipulation: false meeting invitations, open letters, hired prophecy.
- Follows up after launch and does not assume systems will hold on their own.

## Development areas

- **Force in correction.** In Nehemiah 13 he contended with the rulers, cursed men, struck some of them and pulled out their hair. Scripture records this without comment. Data Play should neither celebrate it as decisive leadership nor turn it into comedy. It is serious material about a leader whose zeal could become physical.
- **Anger.** He says he was very angry (5:6) and severely grieved (13:8). In chapter 5 he "consulted with myself" before confronting; in chapter 13 the text records less restraint.
- **Systems that depend on his presence.** In his absence the storerooms, Levites' pay, Sabbath and covenant commitments all drifted (13:6–11).
- **Remembering his own good deeds.** His "remember me" prayers are honest and can be read as healthy dependence on God. They can also read as a man keeping his own ledger. Interpretation varies; do not settle it, but it is fair development material.
- **Hard lines with opponents.** His prayers against Sanballat and Tobiah ask God not to forgive them (4:4–5). This is imprecatory prayer and should be handled as such, not as a model for 2026 staff conflict.
- **Pace.** A fifty-two-day builder can outrun exhausted people. Judah's complaint that the burden bearers' strength was fading (4:10) is part of his story.

## 2026 ministry connections

- **Facilities (head):** cleaning, building support, logistics, transport, site preparation, maintenance projects, keys and doors. He owns the weekly Facilities report.
- **Mentorship (member):** Tuesday nights. Ownership, assessing before announcing, finishing under opposition, and building leaders who keep things running when the head is away (Hanani and Hananiah, Nehemiah 7:2).
- **Lifegroups:** leads a Wednesday Lifegroup and writes its gathering reports.
- **More Than Enough / Victuals:** by connection only. Nehemiah 5 (debt, hunger, restitution) and his open table (5:17–18) give him a voice on how people in need are treated, but distribution belongs to More Than Enough and food to Victuals.
- **Events / Psalmists:** the wall dedication (Nehemiah 12) involved processions, choirs and instruments. Events and Psalmists own celebrations and worship; Facilities owns the site.
- **Greeters and Ushers:** gates, doors and who comes in when (Nehemiah 7:3; 13:19–22) make a natural coordination point with Mordecai's team. Welcome belongs to Greeters and Ushers.
- **Production:** the trumpeter who stayed beside him to rally workers (4:18–20) can support a careful analogy for site communication. Never imply modern equipment existed.

## Humor

Light material is available where the underlying event is not grave:

- extremely specific ownership lists ("next to him… next to them…", Nehemiah 3) applied to a 2026 cleaning rota;
- declining meetings that would take him off the work ("Why should the work cease while I leave it and come down to you?", 6:3). The original invitation was a plot to harm him (6:2); the joke must be about his calendar, not about the danger;
- reclaiming a storeroom that someone else has quietly moved into, with an inventory (13:4–9). Keep the tone on the storeroom, not on the temple desecration;
- "Remember me, my God" appended to a very ordinary Facilities report, which Cris or Ezra may notice in comments;
- tradespeople named by craft (goldsmiths, perfumers, merchants) turning up to a volunteer workday.

Humor must not come from Nehemiah 5 (families selling children into bondage), threats of killing, the violence in 13:25, the covenant-marriage reforms or the grief of chapter 1.

## Sensitivity

- **Economic exploitation** (Nehemiah 5:1–13) includes daughters brought into bondage for debt. Never comedy.
- **Threats of violence** (4:8, 11; 6:2, 10) were real.
- **Imprecatory prayers** (4:4–5; 6:14; 13:29) are biblical prayers against opponents. Present them honestly; do not convert them into 2026 grudges.
- **Mixed marriages and physical correction** (13:23–28) involve families, children's language and Nehemiah striking men. Handle soberly. Do not map it onto modern ethnicity or marriage policy. See also Ezra 9–10 in Ezra's files.
- **Exclusion of Ammonites and Moabites** (13:1–3) should be explained from its text (Deuteronomy 23:3–4) and not used as a modern welcome policy.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| Son of Hacaliah; cupbearer to Artaxerxes in Susa; brother Hanani | Biblical fact (Nehemiah 1:1–2, 11; 2:1; 7:2) |
| "Artaxerxes" is Artaxerxes I, and Nehemiah's arrival is about 445 BC | Widely held scholarly identification, not stated in the text |
| About four months passed between the report and the request | Inference from the months named (Chislev, 1:1; Nisan, 2:1) |
| Nehemiah was a eunuch | Speculation from his court role; not stated. Do not use. |
| Governor of Judah for twelve years (20th to 32nd year of Artaxerxes) | Biblical fact (5:14; 13:6) |
| Nehemiah son of Azbuk (3:16) and the Nehemiah who returned with Zerubbabel (Ezra 2:2; Nehemiah 7:7) | Different people. Do not merge. |
| Nehemiah and Ezra worked in Jerusalem at the same time | Stated in Nehemiah 8:9 and 12:26, 36. The historical order of Ezra's and Nehemiah's missions is debated by scholars (see Ezra's profile). Data Play follows the text without arguing the question. |
| The prayer of Nehemiah 9 was Nehemiah's | Not stated. The text introduces it through the Levites (9:4–5). Do not attribute it to Nehemiah. |
| Nehemiah wrote the book | The first-person memoir sections are presented as his words (1:1). The final form of Ezra–Nehemiah is usually thought to be edited; do not argue it in Data Play. |
| Nehemiah and Esther served in the same palace a generation apart | Inference only. Susa is the setting of both books, and Artaxerxes I is historically Xerxes I's son, but Scripture records no connection. |
| Motives of Sanballat, Tobiah and Geshem | Partly stated: grieved that someone sought Israel's welfare (2:10), angry and mocking (4:1–3), intending harm (6:2). Political reasons beyond the text are inference. |
| His "remember me" prayers express pride or piety | Interpretation; not settled by the text. |
