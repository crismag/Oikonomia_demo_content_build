## Who Apollos is

Apollos was a Jew from Alexandria, "an eloquent man," who came to Ephesus. He "was mighty in the Scriptures," had been instructed in the way of the Lord, was "fervent in spirit," and "spoke and taught accurately the things concerning Jesus, although he knew only the baptism of John" (Acts 18:24–25). When Priscilla and Aquila heard him speak boldly in the synagogue, they took him aside and explained the way of God to him more accurately (18:26).

He then wanted to go to Achaia. The believers encouraged him and wrote to the disciples there to welcome him. In Corinth he "greatly helped those who had believed through grace," publicly and powerfully showing from the Scriptures that Jesus was the Christ (18:27–28; 19:1).

In Corinth, believers later divided into groups: "I follow Paul," "I follow Apollos," "I follow Cephas," "I follow Christ" (1 Corinthians 1:12). Paul refused the rivalry: "I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase" (3:6). Both were servants, and Paul applied the lesson to himself and Apollos so that no one would be puffed up in favour of one against another (4:6). Paul strongly urged Apollos to visit Corinth, but Apollos was not willing to go then and would come when he had the opportunity (16:12). Much later Titus was asked to send "Zenas the lawyer and Apollos" on their way with nothing lacking (Titus 3:13).

Apollos is not the "wrong" teacher who had to be fixed. He was already accurate, fervent and effective, and he was humble enough to receive more. Scripture never blames him for the faction that used his name.

In 2026 he is a gifted teacher in development. He reports to Priscilla, is mentored by her and Aquila at Mentorship Night, and teaches across the church when invited.

## 2026 voice

- Eloquent and energetic. Long, well-built sentences, vivid images, and a natural sense of an audience.
- Scripture-dense. He argues from the Old Testament to Jesus and can connect passages quickly.
- Fervent. He cares visibly about what he is teaching.
- Open to learning, and says so; he asks for feedback after he teaches rather than waiting to be offered it.
- Independent about his own timing and decisions, and courteous in explaining them.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation, and not a TED-talk parody.

He should not sound like Paul (personal, argued correspondence; "not in persuasive words of human wisdom"), Priscilla (precise and brief), Peter (short, plainspoken) or Isaiah (prophetic poetry). His written reports tend to run long, which is a gentle recurring comment thread.

## Strengths

- Eloquence and public boldness (Acts 18:24–26, 28).
- Deep knowledge of the Scriptures (18:24, 28).
- Fervour and accuracy in what he knew (18:25).
- Teachability: an effective public teacher received further instruction from a couple who took him aside (18:26).
- Strengthening believers others had already reached: he "watered" (1 Corinthians 3:6; Acts 18:27).
- Standing as Paul's co-worker, not his rival (1 Corinthians 3:5–9; 16:12).

## Development areas

These are drawn from the shape of his biblical story. They are not recorded failures.

- Continuing to learn once people already admire him (Acts 18:25–26).
- Not letting his name become a rallying point, even when he did nothing to seek it (1 Corinthians 1:12; 3:4–7). Scripture does not say he encouraged the faction.
- Remembering that gifted speech is not the power of the gospel (1 Corinthians 1:17; 2:1–5). This is Paul speaking of his own ministry; applying it to Apollos is inference.
- Using his freedom about timing and invitations in a way that serves the churches (1 Corinthians 16:12). The text does not criticise his decision.

## Sensitivity

- **Incomplete understanding** (Acts 18:25) is not error for which he is blamed. He taught accurately what he knew.
- **Factions** (1 Corinthians 1–4) caused real harm to a church. Records about leader-loyalty problems are serious and often restricted.
- **Hebrews:** some have suggested Apollos wrote the letter to the Hebrews. That is speculation. Data Play does not claim it, and records must not attribute Hebrews to him.
