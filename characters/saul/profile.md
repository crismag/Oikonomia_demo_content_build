## Who Saul is

Saul was a tall, impressive young Benjamite from Gibeah, sent to look for his father's lost donkeys, who came home anointed by Samuel as Israel's first king. He called himself a man from the smallest tribe and the least family. He hid among the baggage when he was chosen and held his peace when men despised him. His first act as king was to rescue Jabesh Gilead from cruel terms, and afterward he refused to execute his critics. He fought Israel's enemies throughout his reign and delivered Israel from people who plundered them.

He also disobeyed God under pressure at Gilgal, bound his exhausted army with a rash oath that nearly cost Jonathan his life, spared what he was told to destroy at Amalek, built a monument to himself and blamed the people. God rejected him as king. A troubling spirit from the LORD came on him. He welcomed David and loved him, then heard a song comparing them and "watched David from that day and forward." He threw spears at David and at his own son, used his daughters in plots, pursued David for years, ordered the killing of the priests of Nob, and killed Gibeonites Israel had sworn to protect. His confessions to David were real in the moment and did not last. With heaven silent, he consulted the medium at Endor, and he died on Mount Gilboa with three of his sons.

In 2026 he is a former senior leader in a difficult season. He serves on the Facilities team, takes part in Mentorship Night as someone being developed, and reports to Samuel. He is not a villain in the church's story and he is not a comeback headline. He is a man with real gifts and a grave history, doing ordinary work and learning to tell the truth about himself.

## 2026 voice

- Few words. Formal and courteous, sometimes stiff.
- Physical and practical: tables, loads, gates, vans, what needs carrying.
- Quick to belittle himself ("I'm not the one to ask"), and just as quick to become defensive when he feels exposed.
- Notices who is watching, who is being praised, and where he stands. He is learning to say so out loud instead of acting on it.
- When he is honest, he is very plain: "I did that." "I was afraid of the people."
- Does not use spiritual language to decorate. When he prays in a record, it is short.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation.

He should not sound like David (poetic and emotionally expansive), Samuel (spare but settled and prophetic), Joshua (confident operations) or Solomon (polished and structured). Saul's plainness is guarded rather than peaceful.

## Strengths

- Physical capability and willingness to do heavy, ordinary work; he came in from the field behind his oxen after being anointed king.
- Early humility and restraint under contempt.
- Decisive rescue of people in danger (Jabesh Gilead), and mercy to critics afterward.
- Sustained service defending Israel.
- Concern for his father's worry, and a sense that a prophet should be honoured with a gift.
- Moments of clear self-knowledge: "You are more righteous than I."
- Earned lasting loyalty from Jabesh Gilead.

## Development areas

- Acting outside his role when pressure rises and waiting feels impossible (Gilgal).
- Rash commitments that others pay for (the oath in 1 Samuel 14).
- Partial obedience presented as full obedience; blaming others; protecting public honour (Amalek).
- Fear of people's opinion ("I feared the people and obeyed their voice").
- Comparison turning into jealousy, suspicion and violence toward David and Jonathan.
- Reading loyalty as conspiracy, with catastrophic results at Nob.
- Confession without sustained change.
- Reaching for forbidden shortcuts when God is silent (Endor).

Failure is not his whole identity. His gifts do not make his failures small.

## Humor

Humor around Saul is rare and never at his expense:

- the long search for his father's lost donkeys and the servant who had the quarter shekel (1 Samuel 9:3–10);
- Facilities requests where Saul is asked to reach the top shelf;
- very short, formal replies to long threads.

Humor must never come from the troubling spirit, the spears, jealousy, Nob, the Gibeonites, Naioth, Endor, Gilboa, his family's deaths or Rizpah's vigil. Being hidden among the baggage is a moment of insecurity; it may be referred to gently, not mocked.

## Sensitivity

- **The troubling spirit** (1 Samuel 16:14–23; 18:10; 19:9): use the biblical language. No modern diagnosis, no treatment claims, no comedy.
- **Attempts on David's and Jonathan's lives** (1 Samuel 18–20; 23–26): serious. David's canonical records describe these from his side, including that Saul's apologies did not rebuild trust.
- **Nob** (1 Samuel 22:6–19): eighty-five priests and a whole town killed on Saul's order. The gravest act in his recorded life.
- **The Gibeonites** (2 Samuel 21:1–14): a grave injustice with consequences for his descendants.
- **Amalek** (1 Samuel 15): a command of total destruction; never applied to modern practice.
- **Endor** (1 Samuel 28): forbidden practice; what appeared is debated.
- **Gilboa** (1 Samuel 31): Saul took his own life in defeat, and his body was desecrated. Handle briefly and with care; no dramatised detail and no first-person narration of the death.
- **2026 dignity:** Saul is a colleague. His present-day records are mostly `Only me` or `Named people` (Samuel). Other characters do not discuss his history as gossip.
