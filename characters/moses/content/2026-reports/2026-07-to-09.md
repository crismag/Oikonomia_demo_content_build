## 2026-07-05 — Twelve Leaders Can See the Same Land Differently

**Record type:** Leadership Assessment Reflection  
**Visibility:** Leadership  
**Status:** Complete

This week we compared several assessments of the same ministry situation. It reminded me of the twelve men sent to explore Canaan.

All twelve saw the land. They brought back evidence of its fruitfulness. They also saw fortified cities and powerful inhabitants. The disagreement was not over whether obstacles existed. The disagreement was over what those obstacles meant in light of what God had promised.

Ten concluded that Israel could not succeed. Caleb urged the people to go forward, and Joshua stood with him. Fear spread through the congregation until people talked about choosing another leader and returning to Egypt.

Reports are never merely collections of facts. People interpret what they see.

I want our leaders to report problems truthfully without treating fear as objectivity. Faith does not require pretending the fortified city is small. It requires refusing to make the fortified city greater than God.

**Connections:** Leadership reporting; decision making; Joshua and Caleb.

**Biblical account:** Numbers 13–14.

## 2026-07-12 — Joshua and Caleb: Minority Reports Still Need to Be Heard

**Record type:** Mentorship Report  
**Visibility:** Leadership  
**Status:** Complete

I spent additional time with Joshua this week reflecting on the report from Canaan and what it revealed about his leadership.

Joshua and Caleb were outnumbered ten to two among the scouts. They did not deny the strength of the people in the land. They insisted that Israel should not rebel against the Lord or fear the inhabitants because the Lord was with us.

The congregation did not reward them for that position. The people talked about stoning them.

Joshua's later leadership cannot be separated from these earlier moments when he learned to remain faithful without majority support.

In 2026 I want to give emerging leaders room to disagree responsibly. A leadership team becomes dangerous when agreement with the most senior voice is mistaken for faithfulness.

**Mentorship note:** Continue giving Joshua decisions where he must exercise judgment rather than merely execute mine.

**Related goal:** Prepare Joshua to lead without requiring him to lead like me.  
**Biblical account:** Numbers 13:25–33; 14:1–10.

## 2026-07-19 — Rebellion Has Consequences

**Record type:** Serious Leadership Reflection  
**Visibility:** Leadership  
**Status:** Complete

I do not want our reflections on leadership difficulty to become so therapeutic that we stop taking rebellion and unbelief seriously.

After the scouts returned, the congregation wept, complained against Aaron and me, and said they wished they had died in Egypt or the wilderness. They proposed choosing another leader and returning to Egypt. Joshua and Caleb pleaded with them not to rebel against the Lord.

God's judgment followed. The generation that had repeatedly refused to trust Him would not enter the land as they had expected, while Joshua and Caleb would.

I interceded for the people, but intercession did not erase every consequence.

Grace, prayer, accountability, and consequence must not be treated as enemies. Leadership should not invent punishment from wounded pride, but neither should it promise that every destructive choice has no consequence.

**Tone:** Serious; no comedy attached to the judgment in this account.

**Biblical account:** Numbers 14.

## 2026-07-26 — Korah and the Seriousness of Leadership Conflict

**Record type:** Leadership Incident Reflection  
**Visibility:** Named people  
**Shared with:** Aaron, Joshua  
**Status:** Complete

This week I reviewed how we distinguish healthy disagreement from a destructive struggle for authority.

Korah, Dathan, Abiram, and others organized a direct challenge against Aaron and me. Their argument used the language of equality and holiness while rejecting the responsibilities God had assigned. The confrontation ended in severe divine judgment and death.

This is not an account I use casually when someone disagrees with me. A leader who labels every critic “Korah” has learned the wrong lesson and may be protecting ego rather than God's order.

At the same time, Scripture does not treat organized rebellion as harmless simply because it uses spiritual language.

I want to remain open to correction—Jethro corrected me and I needed it—while also recognizing that correction, disagreement, ambition, and rebellion are not identical things.

**Tone:** Serious. Do not use the deaths or judgment as humor.

**Biblical account:** Numbers 16.

## 2026-08-02 — The Bronze Serpent: Intercession After Confession

**Record type:** Prayer / Teaching Reflection  
**Visibility:** Shared  
**Status:** Complete

The bronze-serpent account is difficult, but it is important to tell it clearly.

During another stage of the wilderness journey, the people became impatient and spoke against God and against me. Venomous snakes came among them, and many Israelites died. The people came to me, admitted that they had sinned by speaking against the Lord and against me, and asked me to pray that the snakes would be taken away.

I prayed for them.

God instructed me to make a bronze serpent and place it on a pole. Anyone who had been bitten could look at it and live.

I am struck again by the movement from sin, to consequence, to confession, to intercession, to the means of rescue God provided.

I did not need the people to prove that their apology was sufficiently painful before I prayed for them.

**Biblical account:** Numbers 21:4–9.  
**Reflection theme:** Confession and intercession can begin while consequences are still painfully real.

## 2026-08-09 — Meribah: Being Right About the Problem Did Not Make My Response Right

**Record type:** Personal Development  
**Visibility:** Named people  
**Shared with:** Aaron, Joshua  
**Status:** Attention Required

I returned this week to one of the failures I most need to remember.

At Meribah the congregation had no water and quarreled with Aaron and me. The need was real and the complaints were intense. God told me to take the staff, gather the assembly, and speak to the rock before them. He promised that water would come from it.

Instead, I stood before the people angry and said, “Listen, you rebels...” Then I struck the rock twice.

Water still came.

That detail has always humbled me. The people received what they needed, but the successful outcome did not mean my leadership response was right. God dealt seriously with Aaron and me because we had failed to honor Him as holy before the people.

I was correct that the situation was serious. I was not correct in how I carried my anger into it.

Experience does not excuse me from self-control. If anything, it increases my responsibility.

**Attention:** Review recent tone and accumulated frustration with mentor/accountability leaders.  
**Related goal:** Respond to frustration without allowing anger to lead me.  
**Biblical account:** Numbers 20:1–13.

## 2026-08-16 — Accountability After Anger

**Record type:** Personal Development Follow-up  
**Visibility:** Named people  
**Shared with:** Aaron, Joshua  
**Status:** In Progress

Following last week's Meribah reflection, I reviewed several recent conversations where my concern was legitimate but my tone was sharper than necessary.

My temptation is to defend the substance of what I said. That is not the question I need to answer.

At Meribah, water came from the rock. If I had evaluated myself only by the result, I could have argued that the problem was solved. God evaluated something deeper: whether I had trusted Him and honored Him before the people.

I have followed up where needed and will continue asking trusted leaders to tell me when frustration is becoming visible before I recognize it myself.

I do not want an apology to become a ritual that allows the same pattern to continue indefinitely. The goal is changed leadership behavior.

**Progress:** Follow-up underway.

**Related goal:** Respond to frustration without allowing anger to lead me.

## 2026-08-23 — Aaron: Grief and Transition

**Record type:** Personal / Leadership Reflection  
**Visibility:** Named people  
**Shared with:** Aaron, Miriam  
**Status:** Complete

I have been thinking about Aaron not merely as a ministry colleague but as my brother.

He stood beside me when I was afraid of speaking. He confronted Pharaoh with me. He made serious mistakes, including his role in the golden calf. We disagreed. He was corrected. He served as high priest. He carried responsibilities I did not carry, and I carried responsibilities he did not carry.

Near Mount Hor, God told Aaron, Eleazar, and me to go up the mountain. Aaron's priestly garments were transferred to his son Eleazar, and Aaron died there. When the congregation learned of his death, they mourned him for thirty days.

Leadership transition can be written in terms of roles, responsibilities, successors, and continuity. Those things matter. But sometimes the person leaving the role is also your brother, your friend, or someone who has stood beside you through decades of history.

A clean succession plan does not make grief unnecessary.

**Biblical account:** Numbers 20:22–29.  
**Reflection theme:** Honor transition without reducing people to positions.

## 2026-08-30 — Giving Joshua Authority, Not Merely More Work

**Record type:** Mentorship / Succession Report  
**Visibility:** Leadership  
**Status:** In Progress

Joshua has taken on additional leadership responsibility this month. My work now is partly to avoid interfering with the authority I say I want him to exercise.

When God told me that I would not lead Israel into the land, I asked Him to appoint someone over the community so the people would not be like sheep without a shepherd. God identified Joshua. I brought him before Eleazar and the congregation, laid my hands on him, and publicly commissioned him. Later I was instructed to strengthen and encourage him because he would lead the people forward.

That history makes one thing clear: succession is not giving someone all the work while preserving all the authority for yourself.

I will continue redirecting routine attempts to bypass Joshua. Mentorship remains available, but it should help him lead rather than train him to wait for my answer.

**Progress:** Additional decisions transferred to Joshua.

**Related goal:** Prepare Joshua to lead without requiring him to lead like me.  
**Biblical account:** Numbers 27:15–23; Deuteronomy 31:1–8, 23.

## 2026-09-06 — Remembering the Journey Truthfully

**Record type:** Testimony / Leadership Reflection  
**Visibility:** Shared  
**Status:** Complete

As we approach the final part of this year's active reporting period, I have been reviewing how we tell our history.

Near the end of my leadership of Israel, I retold the journey to a generation that needed to understand where we had come from. I did not tell a polished story in which our leaders were always wise and the people were always faithful.

I remembered God's deliverance from Egypt. I remembered fear, rebellion, provision, judgment, intercession, victories, failures, and the years in the wilderness. I also did not erase my own failure from the story.

Institutional memory becomes dangerous when it exists mainly to protect the institution's reputation.

Our testimony should make God's faithfulness clearer, not make our own history prettier.

**Biblical account:** Deuteronomy 1–4 and the wider reflections throughout Deuteronomy.  
**Reflection theme:** Tell the truth about the journey so the next generation can learn from it.

## 2026-09-13 — Teach the Next Generation Why

**Record type:** Teaching / Ministry Reflection  
**Visibility:** Shared  
**Status:** Complete

I spent part of this week thinking about what our children should inherit from us besides activities and schedules.

I taught Israel to love the Lord with all their heart, soul, and strength and to keep His commands on their hearts. They were to teach these things diligently to their children—at home, while traveling, when lying down, and when getting up.

The goal was not for children merely to recognize religious vocabulary. They were to grow up inside a community that continually explained who God is, what He had done, and how His people should live.

This is why Gifts and Arrows matters beyond childcare. Caring for children safely and lovingly is essential, but so is helping them understand the faith of the community they are growing within.

**Related ministry:** Gifts and Arrows  
**Ministry connection:** Gifts and Arrows.

**Biblical account:** Deuteronomy 6:4–9; compare Exodus 12:26–27.

## 2026-09-20 — Commissioning Joshua

**Record type:** Leadership Transition Report  
**Visibility:** Shared  
**Status:** Milestone Complete

This week's focus is not on whether Joshua can imitate me. It is on whether he is prepared to lead faithfully in the work God has given him.

When God identified Joshua as the man who would lead after me, the transition was made visible before the community. I laid my hands on him and commissioned him. Later, before Israel entered the land, I told him to be strong and courageous because the Lord would go with him and would not leave or forsake him.

Public leadership transition matters because the community needs to know whom it should follow. Private mentoring alone is not enough if public authority remains ambiguous.

In our 2026 work I want to make responsibility clear enough that people do not need to guess whether Joshua's decisions are real or provisional until Moses comments.

**Milestone:** Joshua's defined authority reaffirmed publicly.

**Related goal:** Prepare Joshua to lead without requiring him to lead like me.

## 2026-09-27 — Finishing Faithfully

**Record type:** Leadership Progress / Testimony  
**Visibility:** Shared  
**Status:** Complete

My 2026 goals have increasingly forced me to think about what it means to finish a season well.

At the end of my biblical journey, God allowed me to see the land Israel would enter, but I did not enter it with them. Joshua would lead the people forward.

That ending has never allowed me to define faithfulness as personally completing every chapter I helped begin.

Scripture also records something I appreciate for practical reasons: when I was 120 years old, my eyesight had not become weak and my strength had not gone. The issue was not that I had become incapable of seeing the work ahead. The issue was that the next part belonged to Joshua.

There is a difference between being unable to continue and recognizing that another leader is supposed to continue.

This year I have tried to reduce unnecessary decisions coming to me, give Joshua genuine authority, respond more carefully when frustration accumulates, and protect time for God's presence. I have not done all of that perfectly. But I can see progress.

Finishing faithfully is not disappearing. It is also not remaining indispensable on purpose.

**2026 progress:** Continue transition work into the future timeline.  
**Note:** Deuteronomy 34:7 specifically states that Moses' eyesight was not weak and his strength had not gone.

**Biblical account:** Deuteronomy 31; Deuteronomy 34.
