# Isaiah — Biblical Context Library

This file records Isaiah's lived biblical history, and the core teaching of the book that bears his name, as they may surface in 2026 Oikonomia content. These are not 2026 reenactments.

Isaiah's recorded life events are few; his message is vast. Records should keep the two distinct: **narrative memories** (what Isaiah did and saw) and **teaching** (what the book of Isaiah says). Because the authorship of chapters 40–66 is debated, Isaiah may teach from them but does not present their historical setting (exile, Cyrus, return) as his own memory.

When an account is used in a record, explain enough that a reader who does not know the Bible can follow who was there, what happened and why it mattered.

Quotations follow the World English Bible (WEB). Where a statement is interpretation or tradition rather than explicit text, it is marked.

## Part 1 — Isaiah's life

### The setting — Isaiah 1:1; 2 Kings 15–20

Isaiah prophesied in Judah, the southern kingdom, across four kings: Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah. In this period Assyria was the dominant empire. It destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel and its capital Samaria (2 Kings 17), and later invaded Judah.

**2026 use:** Background for every Isaiah record. Explain briefly.

### The throne-room vision and call — Isaiah 6

"In the year that King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord sitting on a throne, high and lifted up; and his train filled the temple." Seraphim, each with six wings, covered their faces and feet and called to one another, "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of Armies! The whole earth is full of his glory!" The thresholds shook and the house filled with smoke.

Isaiah said, "Woe is me! For I am undone, because I am a man of unclean lips and I live among a people of unclean lips, for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of Armies!" A seraph flew to him with a live coal from the altar, touched his mouth, and said his iniquity was taken away and his sin forgiven. Then Isaiah heard the Lord say, "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" He answered, "Here I am. Send me!"

The assignment was hard: to speak to a people who would hear but not understand. Isaiah asked, "Lord, how long?" The answer was until the land was desolate and the people removed. Even a remaining tenth would be burned, "as a terebinth, and as an oak whose stump remains when they are cut down, so the holy seed is its stump."

**Interpretation:** Whether this is Isaiah's first call or a later commissioning is debated. The meaning of "make the heart of this people fat" (6:10) is discussed; Jesus and Paul quote it about unbelief (Matthew 13:14–15; Acts 28:25–27).

**Truths:** Seeing God's holiness exposed Isaiah's own speech first. Cleansing came before sending. He volunteered before hearing how hard the task would be, and God did not promise success. A stump can hold holy seed.

**2026 use:** Testimony, preparing to teach, Mentorship for emerging teachers, Lifegroup on holiness and calling, perseverance when people do not respond.

### Ahaz at the conduit: "keep calm" — Isaiah 7:1–12

When the kings of Syria and Israel marched against Jerusalem, King Ahaz's heart and his people's hearts "trembled … as the trees of the forest tremble with the wind." God sent Isaiah, with his son Shear-jashub, to meet Ahaz at the end of the conduit of the upper pool. The message was: "Be careful, and keep calm. Don't be afraid, neither let your heart be faint because of these two tails of smoking torches." Their plot would not stand. "If you will not believe, surely you shall not be established."

God invited Ahaz to ask for a sign, as deep as the depths or as high as the heights. Ahaz said, "I won't ask. I won't tempt the LORD."

**Context:** 2 Kings 16:7–9 records that Ahaz sent silver and gold to the king of Assyria for help instead.

**Truths:** Fear shakes whole communities, leaders first. Isaiah's counsel was calm trust, not a political alliance. Pious-sounding language ("I won't tempt the LORD") can cover a decision already made.

**2026 use:** Leadership counsel, crisis meetings, Lifegroup on fear. Isaiah advises; the leader decides.

### The Immanuel sign — Isaiah 7:13–25

Isaiah answered Ahaz, "Listen now, house of David. Is it not enough for you to try the patience of men, that you will try the patience of my God also? Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Behold, the virgin will conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel." Before the child knew right from wrong, the lands of the two kings Ahaz feared would be deserted. But Assyria, the power Ahaz trusted, would devastate Judah too.

**Interpretation:** The Hebrew word translated "virgin" in the WEB is translated "young woman" in some versions. Many see a sign fulfilled in Ahaz's time and a fuller fulfilment in Jesus' birth, which Matthew 1:22–23 states. Isaiah 8:8 addresses the land as "O Immanuel." Data Play does not settle the details.

**Truths:** God gave a sign to a leader who refused to ask. "God with us" came with a warning about misplaced trust.

**2026 use:** Christmas-season teaching, carefully explained. Isaiah does not claim to remember Jesus' birth.

### Maher-shalal-hash-baz and the sealed teaching — Isaiah 8

God told Isaiah to write "For Maher Shalal Hash Baz" on a large tablet, with Uriah the priest and Zechariah son of Jeberechiah as witnesses. Isaiah "went to the prophetess, and she conceived, and bore a son," and God told him to give the child that name. Before the child could say "My father" or "My mother," Assyria would plunder Damascus and Samaria.

God warned Isaiah not to walk in the people's way or share their fear: "The LORD of Armies is who you must respect as holy. He is the one you must fear." Isaiah said, "Wrap up the covenant. Seal the law among my disciples. I will wait for the LORD, who hides his face from the house of Jacob, and I will look for him. Behold, I and the children whom the LORD has given me are for signs and for wonders in Israel." He warned against consulting mediums: "Should they consult the dead on behalf of the living? Turn to the law and to the covenant!"

**Note:** "The prophetess" is commonly understood to be Isaiah's wife; the text does not use that word. Name meaning (roughly "swift to the plunder, quick to the spoil") is a translation of the Hebrew.

**Truths:** Isaiah's family life was part of his message. He committed the message to disciples and chose to wait for God when God seemed hidden. Fear of God displaces fear of conspiracies.

**2026 use:** Teaching and documentation (a witnessed record), family and ministry, discipleship, waiting through silence.

### Walking naked and barefoot — Isaiah 20

In the year the Assyrian commander captured the Philistine city of Ashdod, God told Isaiah to take off the sackcloth from his waist and the sandals from his feet. "He did so, walking naked and barefoot." God said Isaiah had done this "three years for a sign and a wonder concerning Egypt and concerning Ethiopia": Assyria would lead away Egyptian and Ethiopian captives, young and old, "naked and barefoot," to their shame. Those who had looked to Egypt and Ethiopia for rescue from Assyria would ask, "how will we escape?"

**Interpretation:** "Naked" is widely understood as stripped to a minimal garment, as captives were; the text does not specify. Whether the sign lasted three full years or was performed across three years is discussed.

**Truths:** Isaiah embodied the humiliation of trusting the wrong rescuer, at personal cost, for a long time.

**2026 use:** Serious teaching on misplaced trust; the cost of obedience. Restraint only; no nudity or dress-code humor.

### Shebna and Eliakim — Isaiah 22:15–25

God sent Isaiah to Shebna, the steward over the royal household, who was cutting himself an elaborate tomb in the rock. Isaiah announced that Shebna would be thrown from his office and that Eliakim son of Hilkiah would be clothed with his robe and given "the key of David's house," to be "a father to the inhabitants of Jerusalem." The oracle ends with a warning that even that secure peg would one day give way.

**Note:** In Isaiah 36–37, Eliakim is "over the household" and Shebna is "the scribe," which fits at least part of this oracle. The details of the transition are not narrated.

**Truths:** Leaders can build monuments to themselves. Office is entrusted, and even good appointments are not permanent.

**2026 use:** Restricted leadership counsel on self-promotion and stewardship; succession. Serious.

### Hezekiah and the Assyrian siege — Isaiah 36–37; 2 Kings 18–19; 2 Chronicles 32:1–23

In Hezekiah's fourteenth year Sennacherib of Assyria captured Judah's fortified cities and sent his official, the Rabshakeh, with an army to Jerusalem. He mocked Hezekiah's trust in Egypt and in the LORD, spoke in Hebrew so the people on the wall could hear, and claimed no god had saved any nation from Assyria. Hezekiah tore his clothes, put on sackcloth, went into the temple, and sent Eliakim, Shebna and the elders to Isaiah: "Today is a day of trouble … lift up your prayer for the remnant that is left."

Isaiah replied: "Tell your master, 'The LORD says, "Don't be afraid of the words that you have heard, with which the servants of the king of Assyria have blasphemed me."'" Sennacherib would hear a report and return home, and fall by the sword there.

Sennacherib then sent Hezekiah a threatening letter. Hezekiah spread it before the LORD in the temple and prayed. Isaiah sent Hezekiah God's answer: "Because you have prayed to me against Sennacherib king of Assyria …" The virgin daughter of Zion despised the Assyrian king; God would put a hook in his nose and turn him back; a remnant would again take root and bear fruit; the king of Assyria would not enter the city or shoot an arrow there. That night the angel of the LORD struck 185,000 in the Assyrian camp. Sennacherib returned to Nineveh and was later killed by his sons while worshipping.

**Chronicles adds:** "Hezekiah the king and Isaiah the prophet, the son of Amoz, prayed because of this, and cried to heaven" (2 Chronicles 32:20).

**Truths:** Hostile words were meant to break a city's courage; Isaiah's first word was "Don't be afraid." God answered Hezekiah's prayer explicitly because he prayed. Prophet and king prayed together.

**2026 use:** Crisis counsel, prayer meetings, hostile communications (treated seriously), Hezekiah relationship. The deaths in the Assyrian camp and Sennacherib's murder are not triumphalist material.

### Hezekiah's illness — Isaiah 38; 2 Kings 20:1–11

Hezekiah became mortally ill. Isaiah came and told him, "The LORD says, 'Set your house in order, for you will die, and not live.'" Hezekiah turned his face to the wall, prayed and wept bitterly. In 2 Kings, before Isaiah had left the middle part of the city, God's word came to him to turn back and tell Hezekiah: "I have heard your prayer. I have seen your tears." God would add fifteen years to his life and defend the city. Isaiah told them to apply a cake of figs to the boil. As a sign, the shadow on the sundial of Ahaz went back ten steps. Hezekiah afterwards wrote a psalm of thanksgiving.

**Truths:** Isaiah delivered a terminal word honestly and then went back with a different one when God sent him. He paired a promise with practical care (the figs).

**2026 use:** Serious pastoral support in illness, honesty with a dying person, prayer, testimony. Never reenact a healing sign in 2026. Field Ministry owns hospital visitation; Isaiah may accompany when asked.

### The Babylonian envoys — Isaiah 39; 2 Kings 20:12–19; 2 Chronicles 32:25–31

Merodach-baladan king of Babylon sent letters and a gift after hearing Hezekiah had recovered. Hezekiah was pleased and showed the envoys everything: silver, gold, spices, oil, his armoury and all his treasures. Isaiah came and asked, "What did these men say? From where did they come to you?" and "What have they seen in your house?" Hezekiah said they had seen everything. Isaiah announced that all of it would one day be carried to Babylon, and some of Hezekiah's own descendants would serve in the Babylonian palace. Hezekiah said, "The LORD's word which you have spoken is good," thinking there would at least be peace and truth in his own days.

**Chronicles adds:** Hezekiah's heart was lifted up after his recovery; he later humbled himself; and in the matter of the envoys, "God left him to test him, that he might know all that was in his heart" (2 Chronicles 32:25–26, 31).

**Truths:** The most dangerous moment can follow a deliverance. Isaiah asked plain questions before he gave the verdict. Relief that trouble will come after one's own lifetime is not the same as care for the next generation.

**2026 use:** Restricted leadership counsel after success, disclosure and security, next-generation thinking, Hezekiah relationship. Serious; not a joke at Hezekiah's expense.

## Part 2 — Core teaching in the book of Isaiah

These are not events in Isaiah's life. They are themes Isaiah teaches in 2026. Quote accurately and keep the context.

### Worship joined to justice — Isaiah 1:10–18; 58:1–12

God rejected sacrifices, festivals and many prayers from people whose "hands are full of blood," and called them to "Learn to do well. Seek justice. Relieve the oppressed. Defend the fatherless. Plead for the widow." He also invited them: "Come now, and let's reason together … Though your sins are as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow." In chapter 58 people complained that God ignored their fasting while they oppressed their workers and quarrelled. God described the fast He chooses: to loosen the bonds of wickedness, "to distribute your bread to the hungry, and that you bring the poor who are cast out to your house," to clothe the naked. Then "your light will break out as the morning."

**2026 use:** Teaching, Lifegroup, fasting reflection, More Than Enough connection (operations stay with MTE). Avoid turning 58 into a slogan.

### The vineyard song — Isaiah 5:1–7

A song of a well-tended vineyard that yielded only wild grapes. "He looked for justice, but behold, oppression, for righteousness, but behold, a cry of distress."

**2026 use:** Teaching on fruitfulness and accountability; one of Isaiah's images for ministry reviews, used seriously.

### The coming king and peace — Isaiah 2:2–4; 9:1–7; 11:1–9

Nations streaming to the mountain of the LORD's house to learn His ways; swords beaten into plowshares. "The people who walked in darkness have seen a great light … For a child is born to us. A son is given to us; and the government will be on his shoulders. His name will be called Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace." A shoot from the stump of Jesse on whom the LORD's Spirit rests, who judges the poor with righteousness; wolf and lamb together.

**Interpretation:** Christians read these as fulfilled in Jesus (Matthew 4:14–16 cites 9:1–2). Isaiah teaches this in 2026 without claiming historical knowledge of how it would be fulfilled.

**2026 use:** Advent and Christmas teaching, hope in dark seasons, Lifegroup.

### Trust in quietness, not in Egypt — Isaiah 30:1–17; 31:1–3

Woe to those who went to Egypt for help without consulting God. The people told the prophets, "Don't prophesy to us right things. Tell us pleasant things." God said, "You will be saved in returning and rest. Your strength will be in quietness and in confidence." "You refused."

**2026 use:** Leadership counsel on panic decisions, pressure to hear only pleasant things.

### Comfort and the greatness of God — Isaiah 40

"'Comfort, comfort my people,' says your God." A voice calls to prepare the way of the LORD in the wilderness. "The grass withers, the flower fades; but the word of our God stands forever." God will "feed his flock like a shepherd" and "gently lead those who have their young." He "doesn't faint. He isn't weary … but those who wait for the LORD will renew their strength. They will mount up with wings like eagles. They will run, and not be weary. They will walk, and not faint."

**Note:** The Gospels apply 40:3 to John the Baptist (Matthew 3:3; John 1:23).

**2026 use:** Pastoral comfort, wellbeing, teaching. Don't use 40:31 as a generic motivational poster; keep it about waiting for the LORD.

### The servant passages — Isaiah 42:1–4; 49:1–6; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12

A servant who will not break a bruised reed; a light to the nations; one who gave his back to those who struck him; one "despised and rejected by men," who "was pierced for our transgressions," "crushed for our iniquities," and on whom "the LORD has laid … the iniquity of us all," who "bore the sins of many and made intercession for the transgressors."

**Interpretation:** The servant is identified in different passages and traditions as Israel, a faithful remnant, the prophet or a future individual. The New Testament applies these passages to Jesus: Philip explained Isaiah 53 to the Ethiopian official by preaching Jesus (Acts 8:32–35); Matthew 8:17 cites 53:4.

**Boundary:** Isaiah must not describe himself as the servant, and must not claim memory of Philip, the Ethiopian, or Jesus reading his scroll in Nazareth (Luke 4:16–21). He may teach, as a 2026 church teacher, how the church reads these passages.

**2026 use:** Easter and Good Friday teaching, Lifegroup, Reach-Out conversations (Philip the Evangelist relationship, fiction). Handle with reverence.

### Good news to the humble — Isaiah 61:1–3

"The Lord GOD's Spirit is on me, because the LORD has anointed me to preach good news to the humble. He has sent me to bind up the broken hearted, to proclaim liberty to the captives…" Jesus read a form of this passage in Nazareth and said, "Today, this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:16–21).

**2026 use:** Teaching on mission and care. Isaiah has no memory of Nazareth.

## Later biblical references to Isaiah

- **2 Chronicles 26:22; 32:20, 32** — recorded the acts of Uzziah and Hezekiah; prayed with Hezekiah.
- **Gospels** — John the Baptist and the voice in the wilderness (Matthew 3:3; John 1:23); Jesus quotes Isaiah on hearing without understanding and on lip-service worship (Matthew 13:14–15; 15:7–9); Jesus reads Isaiah 61 (Luke 4:17–21); "Isaiah said these things when he saw his glory, and spoke of him" (John 12:41).
- **Acts** — the Ethiopian official reading Isaiah 53 (Acts 8:28–35); Paul quoting Isaiah 6 in Rome (Acts 28:25–27).
- **Romans** — Paul quotes Isaiah on the remnant, on unbelief and on the Gentiles (Romans 9:27–29; 10:16, 20; 15:12).

**Boundary:** Isaiah has no memory of any New Testament event. In 2026 colleagues may raise them.
