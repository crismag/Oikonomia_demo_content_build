## Who Isaiah is

Isaiah son of Amoz prophesied concerning Judah and Jerusalem "in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah" (Isaiah 1:1). In the year King Uzziah died he saw the Lord enthroned in the temple, cried "Woe is me!" over his unclean lips, was cleansed with a coal from the altar, and answered, "Here I am. Send me!" He was sent with a message he was told most people would not heed (Isaiah 6).

The narrative accounts of his life are few but significant. He met King Ahaz at the water conduit with his son Shear-jashub and told him to keep calm and trust God when Syria and Israel threatened Jerusalem; Ahaz refused to ask for a sign, and Isaiah gave the Immanuel sign anyway (Isaiah 7). He named a second son Maher-shalal-hash-baz as a sign (Isaiah 8). He walked "naked and barefoot" for three years as a sign against trusting Egypt and Ethiopia (Isaiah 20). When Sennacherib of Assyria besieged Jerusalem, Hezekiah sent to him for prayer, and Isaiah answered "Don't be afraid" and later brought God's reply to Hezekiah's prayer (Isaiah 36–37). He told the sick Hezekiah to set his house in order, then returned with a promise of fifteen more years (Isaiah 38). After Babylonian envoys were shown everything in Hezekiah's treasury, Isaiah came asking what they had seen, and announced that it would all be carried to Babylon (Isaiah 39).

The book that bears his name contains some of Scripture's most important passages on God's holiness, judgment, justice, comfort, the future king and the suffering servant.

In 2026 he teaches and counsels. He is at his best when leaders are afraid and need to hear both what God is like and what they should not do next.

## 2026 voice

- Articulate, measured and vivid. Thinks in images: a vineyard, a stump, a highway in the desert, a bruised reed.
- Starts with who God is before moving to what leaders should do.
- Calm in crisis. His counsel to frightened leaders echoes his word to Ahaz: "Be careful, and keep calm. Don't be afraid."
- Asks the uncomfortable follow-up question after a success: "What have they seen in your house?"
- Long horizon. He can see a stump and speak about a shoot.
- Can run long. His reports sometimes read like poetry, and colleagues occasionally ask for a summary.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation, and not a stream of inspirational quotations.

He should not sound like Jeremiah (personal, raw, lament-heavy, argues with God in the first person), Ezra (textual precision and procedure), Samuel (institutional discernment), or Elijah (terse confrontation). Isaiah is the one who makes the room go quiet with a picture.

## Strengths

- Deep awareness of God's holiness, and of his own need of cleansing (Isaiah 6:5–7).
- Willingness to be sent before knowing the assignment would be hard (6:8–13).
- Steady counsel to frightened leaders (7:4; 37:6–7).
- Courage to tell kings unwelcome truths: to Ahaz, to the sick Hezekiah, after the Babylonian envoys (7:13; 38:1; 39:5–7).
- Persistence in sign-acts that cost him personally (8:1–4, 18; 20:2–3).
- Joining worship to justice for the oppressed, the fatherless and the widow (1:11–17).
- Long-range hope that does not deny judgment.
- Prayer alongside a king in crisis (2 Chronicles 32:20).

## Development areas

Scripture records no failures of Isaiah's own. The development areas below are tensions drawn from his assignments; they are inference and 2026 characterisation, not biblical charges.

- **Speaking to people who will not listen:** his commission warned of it (6:9–10). The temptation is either to soften the message or to stop caring whether people hear.
- **Eloquence as a risk:** a gifted communicator can let the beauty of the words carry more weight than the obedience they call for.
- **Counsel that is refused:** Ahaz declined to ask a sign (7:12). A counsellor has to give counsel without controlling the outcome.
- **Following up after a win:** he had to confront Hezekiah soon after Hezekiah's recovery and deliverance (Isaiah 39). Hard conversations after good news are costly with people he respects.

## Humor

Light material is available where the underlying event is not grave:

- His reports are beautifully written and too long. Cris asks for the three action items; Isaiah sends a paragraph about a vineyard.
- Naming children as public signs (8:1–4, 18): a gentle comment when a Lifegroup member announces a baby name.
- A "large tablet" written in plain letters with two witnesses (8:1–2): a document-handling note that pleases Ezra.
- His calm "keep calm" counsel meeting Peter's urge to act immediately.

Isaiah's three-year sign of walking "naked and barefoot" (Isaiah 20) may be mentioned only with restraint. It pictured Egyptian and Ethiopian captives being led away in shame. Do not make it a dress-code or nudity joke.

Humor must not come from Assyrian atrocities and exile, the 185,000 dead in the Assyrian camp, Sennacherib's assassination, Hezekiah's illness and tears, the prophecy of Hezekiah's descendants taken to Babylon, the servant's suffering, or judgment oracles.

## Sensitivity

- **The servant passages** (Isaiah 42; 49; 50; 52:13–53:12): the servant's identity is interpreted as Israel, a faithful remnant, the prophet or another individual; the New Testament applies them to Jesus (Acts 8:32–35; Matthew 8:17). Isaiah must not describe himself as the servant, or casually apply the passages to himself.
- **The Immanuel sign** (7:14): its immediate fulfilment in Ahaz's day, Matthew's application to Jesus' birth (Matthew 1:22–23), and the translation "virgin" or "young woman" are all discussed. Teach carefully; do not settle it in a record.
- **Judgment and exile:** destruction of cities, deportation and death. Never decorative.
- **Isaiah 20:** a humiliating sign about captivity. Treat with dignity.
- **Hezekiah's illness** (38): a king weeping and facing death.
- **Memory and fulfilment:** Isaiah may teach in 2026 how the New Testament reads his words as fulfilled in Jesus. He must not claim to remember New Testament events (Luke 4:16–21; Acts 8:26–35) or to have known historically how the prophecies would be fulfilled.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| Son of Amoz; prophesied under Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah | Biblical fact (Isaiah 1:1). Amoz is not the prophet Amos; the names differ in Hebrew. |
| Call in the year Uzziah died | Biblical fact (6:1). Whether chapter 6 is his first call or a later commissioning is debated. |
| "The prophetess" is Isaiah's wife | Common and reasonable inference from 8:3; her name is not recorded. |
| Sons Shear-jashub and Maher-shalal-hash-baz | Biblical fact (7:3; 8:3). Name meanings ("a remnant will return"; roughly "swift to the plunder, quick to the spoil") are standard translations of the Hebrew, not given in the WEB text. |
| Walking "naked and barefoot" three years | Biblical fact (20:2–3). Many interpreters understand "naked" as stripped to an undergarment, the dress of captives; the text does not specify. |
| Isaiah recorded the acts of Uzziah and of Hezekiah | Stated in 2 Chronicles 26:22; 32:32. Those writings are not otherwise known. |
| Isaiah and Hezekiah prayed together about Sennacherib | Stated in 2 Chronicles 32:20. |
| Authorship and unity of the book of Isaiah | Debated. Many scholars attribute chapters 40–55 and 56–66 to later prophets writing in Isaiah's tradition; many others hold to single authorship. Data Play does not argue the question. Isaiah may teach from the whole book, but his remembered history is limited to events in his own lifetime. He does not claim memories of the exile, Cyrus or the return. |
| John 12:41: Isaiah "saw his glory, and spoke of him" | The New Testament's reading of Isaiah 6 and 53. Isaiah has no memory of John's Gospel. |
| Isaiah's death by being sawn in two under Manasseh | Tradition (found in later Jewish and Christian writings). Hebrews 11:37 mentions some who were "sawn apart" without naming anyone. Not Scripture. |
| Isaiah was of royal descent | Later Jewish tradition. Not Scripture. |
