## Who Miriam is

Miriam was the sister of Aaron and Moses, the daughter of Amram and Jochebed (Numbers 26:59). Scripture calls her "Miriam the prophetess, the sister of Aaron," and she is the first person the Bible calls a prophetess (Exodus 15:20). After Israel crossed the Red Sea and Pharaoh's army was overwhelmed, she took a tambourine, "all the women went out after her with tambourines and with dances," and she led them in singing: "Sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously. He has thrown the horse and his rider into the sea" (Exodus 15:21).

The unnamed sister who watched baby Moses in the reeds and spoke up to Pharaoh's daughter (Exodus 2:4–8) is very often identified as Miriam. That is a reasonable inference, because she is Moses' only named sister, but Exodus 2 does not name her.

Later Miriam and Aaron spoke against Moses, over the Cushite woman he had married and over whether the LORD had spoken only through him (Numbers 12). God rebuked them, and Miriam was struck with leprosy. Aaron pleaded, Moses cried out for her healing, and she was shut outside the camp for seven days while the whole people waited to travel until she was brought back. Moses later told Israel to "remember what the LORD your God did to Miriam" (Deuteronomy 24:9). She died at Kadesh and was buried there (Numbers 20:1). Centuries later, through Micah, God reminded Israel, "I sent before you Moses, Aaron, and Miriam" (Micah 6:4).

Miriam is not only the woman with the tambourine and not only the woman who was struck with leprosy. She was a prophetess, a leader whom God Himself counted among those sent before Israel, a woman others followed into worship, and a sibling whose words against her brother had serious consequences that her whole community felt.

In 2026 she heads Psalmists. She believes praise is a response to what God has done, not entertainment, and she leads people out onto the floor who would never have gone alone.

## 2026 voice

- Bright, direct and energetic. She speaks in short, declarative calls: "Sing." "Get up." "Look what God did."
- Communal. Her instinct is "come with me", not "watch me".
- Practical and exacting about practice, equipment and readiness. Tambourines go back where they belong.
- Honest about people, sometimes too quickly. She is learning to take a concern to the person rather than talk about them.
- Joyful without being shallow; she knows why she is singing.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation, no worship-leader clichés.

She should not sound like David (introspective, poetic, lament and personal prayer), Aaron (fluent, diplomatic, long explanations), Moses (measured governance) or Deborah (judicial counsel). Miriam's praise is short, loud, shared and about God's act; David's psalms are long, personal and searching. The contrast is useful in Psalmists records.

## Strengths

- Leading communal praise in direct response to God's deliverance (Exodus 15:20–21).
- Prophetic calling (Exodus 15:20).
- Drawing others, especially women, into worship: "all the women went out after her."
- Recognised leadership: Micah 6:4 names her with Moses and Aaron as sent before Israel.
- If the Exodus 2 identification is right: courage, watchfulness and quick initiative in a dangerous moment.
- Restoration: she was brought back in, and the community waited for her.

## Development areas

- Speaking against a leader instead of to him (Numbers 12:1–2).
- Letting a stated grievance carry a deeper question about status and voice ("Hasn't he spoken also with us?").
- Using her influence to bring someone else (Aaron) along into a complaint.
- Standards that can become impatience with people who are not yet ready.
- The risk of being eclipsed by, or competing with, other gifted worship voices. In 2026 David serves as a leader within the ministry she heads.

Numbers 12 is not her identity. Her song does not erase Numbers 12.

## Humor

Light material is available where the underlying event is not grave:

- tambourine inventory and returning equipment to the right cupboard (already in Moses' 2026-02-15 record);
- her two-line refrain versus David's much longer songs, in song-review comment threads;
- dancers needing more floor than Facilities planned for;
- rehearsals that turn into worship before the run-through is finished;
- "Sing" as a complete set-list note.

Humor must not come from Numbers 12, her leprosy and exclusion, the seven-day wait, her death, or the drowning of Pharaoh's army. Praise is response to God, not entertainment. Miriam is never written as a shrew, a jealous sibling stereotype or a comic diva.

## Sensitivity

- **Numbers 12:** a serious skin disease (WEB "leprous"), public exclusion for seven days, and her brothers' pleading. Serious. It may be written as correction and restoration, never as humiliation or comedy.
- **The Cushite woman** (Numbers 12:1): Scripture records the complaint without explaining it. Do not build ethnic or racial commentary into Miriam's character, and do not invent the woman's story or identity.
- **The Nile and Pharaoh's decree** (Exodus 1:22–2:10): infanticide under oppression. Serious background.
- **The Red Sea:** Egyptians died. The song celebrates God's deliverance; do not turn their deaths into spectacle.
- **Her death** (Numbers 20:1): brief and unadorned in Scripture; do not invent details.

|---|---|
| Sister of Aaron and Moses; daughter of Amram and Jochebed | Biblical fact (Numbers 26:59; 1 Chronicles 6:3). Exodus 15:20 calls her "the sister of Aaron". |
| The sister who watched Moses at the Nile (Exodus 2:4–8) was Miriam | Reasonable inference and common identification. Exodus 2 does not name her. Use sparingly. If a record uses it as Miriam's memory, add a visible note (for example after **Biblical account:**) that Exodus 2 does not name the sister. Never make it the foundation of a goal or a central testimony. |
| Older than Moses | Inference, dependent on the Exodus 2 identification. Her age is not stated. |
| First woman called a prophetess | Biblical fact (first use of the title, Exodus 15:20) |
| Content of her prophetic ministry beyond Exodus 15:21 and the claim in Numbers 12:2 | Not recorded |
| Miriam initiated the Numbers 12 complaint | Inference. She is named first, and only she was struck. Scripture does not say she started it. |
| Identity of the Cushite woman (Zipporah or another wife) | Uncertain. Do not settle it. |
| Why only Miriam was struck | Not stated. Interpretations vary. |
| Miriam's own words of repentance | Not recorded. Aaron's plea, "we have sinned", includes her (Numbers 12:11). |
| She was healed | Strong inference: she was "brought in again" after the seven days (Numbers 12:14–15). |
| Husband or children | Not recorded. Jewish tradition variously links her with Hur or with Caleb. That is tradition, not Scripture; Data Play does not use it. |
| A miraculous well that followed Israel because of Miriam | Rabbinic tradition, sometimes linked to the water shortage right after her death (Numbers 20:1–2). Not Scripture; do not present it as fact. |
| Mourning at her death | Not recorded (contrast thirty days for Aaron and for Moses). Do not claim that no one mourned; Scripture is silent. |
| The Miriam of 1 Chronicles 4:17 | A different person (namesake). Do not merge. |
| "Mary" in the New Testament is the Greek form of the same name | Widely accepted linguistic note. It does not connect Miriam to any Mary historically. |
