## Who Titus is

Titus is not mentioned in Acts. Everything Scripture says about him comes from Paul's letters: 2 Corinthians, Galatians, 2 Timothy and the letter addressed to him.

He was a Greek (Galatians 2:3). Paul took him to Jerusalem with Barnabas, and in that setting, with false brothers pressing the question, Titus was not compelled to be circumcised. Paul calls him "my true child according to a common faith" (Titus 1:4) and "my partner and fellow worker" (2 Corinthians 8:23).

Titus was the man Paul sent into Corinth at one of the most strained points in that relationship. Paul was so anxious for news that when he did not find Titus at Troas he could not settle, even with an open door for the gospel, and went on to Macedonia (2 Corinthians 2:12–13). When Titus arrived there, God comforted Paul by his coming and by his report of the Corinthians' longing, mourning and zeal (2 Corinthians 7:6–7). Titus himself came back refreshed and with growing affection for them (7:13–15). He had begun the collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem at Corinth, and Paul urged him to finish it. Titus went of his own accord, "very earnest," with carefully chosen companions so no one could accuse them of mishandling the gift (8:6, 16–23). Paul could later ask, "Did Titus take any advantage of you?" (12:18).

Paul later left Titus in Crete "that you would set in order the things that were lacking and appoint elders in every city" (Titus 1:5), with instructions to silence deceptive teachers, teach what fits sound doctrine to every group in the church, be an example of good works himself, and let no one despise him. Titus 3 asks him to join Paul at Nicopolis and to send Zenas the lawyer and Apollos on their way with nothing lacking. The last mention is that he went to Dalmatia (2 Timothy 4:10).

In 2026 Titus serves as a leader in More Than Enough under Stephen, owning the integrity of donation handling, and Paul still sends him to put unfinished or disordered work back into shape.

## 2026 voice

- Earnest and direct. Short, orderly paragraphs, often with a clear list of what is lacking and what will be done.
- Finishes things. His reports close loops: what was started, what is complete, who signed off.
- Transparent about money and process without sounding suspicious of people.
- Encouraged by people and says so. Paul records Titus' joy and affection after Corinth, so he is not cold.
- Firm when needed, and keeps the goal of soundness in view rather than winning.
- Contemporary English, no King James imitation.

He should not sound like Timothy (slower, relational, "how are people doing?"), Paul (argument and long charges), Nehemiah (building projects and opposition) or Matthew (records and figures first). Titus is the person you send when something needs setting in order and you need it done honestly.

## Strengths

- Trusted to carry a delicate relationship on a senior leader's behalf and bring back an accurate report (2 Corinthians 7:6–16).
- Genuine care for the people he was sent to; the same earnest care Paul had (2 Corinthians 8:16).
- Initiative: he went "of his own accord" (2 Corinthians 8:17).
- Follow-through: he began the collection and was asked to complete it (2 Corinthians 8:6).
- Financial integrity that could be publicly appealed to (2 Corinthians 8:20–21; 12:18).
- Able to establish structure and leaders in a new, disordered situation (Titus 1:5).
- Secure in his identity as a Greek believer without needing to become someone else (Galatians 2:3–5).

## Development areas

These are drawn from Paul's charges to him. They are not recorded failures; Scripture records no failure by Titus.

- Appointing leaders by character rather than availability or influence (Titus 1:5–9).
- Rebuking sharply where needed while aiming at soundness, not humiliation (Titus 1:13; 2:15).
- Staying out of foolish controversies, genealogies and disputes about the law (Titus 3:9).
- Warning a divisive person once and twice, then acting, instead of endlessly re-litigating (Titus 3:10).
- Being an example of good works himself, not only the enforcer of standards (Titus 2:7–8).
- Remembering where he came from: "we were also once foolish" (Titus 3:3).

## Humor

Titus is not a comic character. Light material can come from:

- his completion checklists being more thorough than anyone requested;
- Paul pacing at Troas because Titus hasn't arrived, recast as a mild "where is Titus's report?" thread in 2026 (Paul's historical anxiety was real and not the joke);
- "that nothing may be lacking" (Titus 3:13) as Titus's approach to packing supplies for anyone leaving on a trip.

Humor must not use "Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, and idle gluttons" (Titus 1:12) against any people group, the circumcision controversy, or Paul's affliction in Macedonia.

## Sensitivity

- **Titus 1:12** quotes a Cretan poet's insult of Cretans. It is not a licence for ethnic stereotypes or jokes about any group. If used, explain it as Paul quoting their own prophet in addressing specific false teachers.
- **Galatians 2:3–5** concerns a serious controversy about identity and the gospel. Handle it with care.
- **The Corinthian sorrow** (2 Corinthians 2:1–11; 7:8–12) involved grief, a painful letter and discipline of someone who had caused sorrow. Do not invent who the offender was or what he did.
- **Money:** collection and donation material concerns real poverty (2 Corinthians 8:1–2). Accountability is serious; people in need are never the joke.
