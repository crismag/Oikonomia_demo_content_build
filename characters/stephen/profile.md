## Who Stephen is

Stephen appears in Scripture in only two chapters, but they are among the most consequential in Acts. When the Jerusalem church grew, Greek-speaking believers (the Hellenists) complained that their widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution. The Twelve asked the believers to choose seven men "of good report, full of the Holy Spirit and of wisdom" to take charge of the work. Stephen is named first, as "a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit" (Acts 6:3–5).

He did more than serve tables. He performed wonders and signs, and men from several synagogues who argued with him could not withstand the wisdom and the Spirit with which he spoke. They then induced false witnesses, had him brought before the council and accused him of speaking against the temple and the law. His answer (Acts 7) is the longest speech in Acts: a retelling of Israel's history from Abraham through Joseph, Moses, the tabernacle, David and Solomon, which ends by charging his hearers with resisting the Holy Spirit as their ancestors had. He saw Jesus standing at God's right hand. He was dragged out of the city and stoned, praying for his killers as he died. A young man named Saul guarded the witnesses' garments and approved of his death (Acts 7:54–8:1).

Scripture records nothing about his age, family, occupation or background beyond this. It never describes him actually running the distribution; it records his appointment to it. His Data Play role as head of More Than Enough rests on that appointment and on the qualities the church looked for, not on invented operational history.

In 2026 he leads More Than Enough. He cares that nobody is overlooked, especially people whose language or background makes them easy to miss. He chooses volunteers for character before availability. He is calm, exact and fair, and he still knows the Scriptures well enough to tell the whole story from the beginning when a moment calls for it.

## 2026 voice

- Calm, measured and precise. He does not raise his voice in writing, even when he is naming something wrong.
- Fairness is his first question: who is not being served, and why?
- Builds from the long view. When he explains a decision he often places it inside a larger pattern, sometimes a biblical one, and he keeps it brief in operational records.
- Gracious toward critics and plain about facts. He will describe a complaint accurately rather than defensively, because the church's first response to the widows' complaint was to take it seriously.
- Does not dramatise himself. Reports are about the people served and the team serving.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation.

He should not sound like Peter (quick, physical, first to speak), Paul (argument and correspondence), Moses (governance and weariness), Matthew (records and figures) or Philip the Evangelist (on the move, one person at a time). Stephen is the steady one with the long memory of Scripture and a short tolerance for unfairness.

## Strengths

- Trusted by a whole community when trust had been strained (Acts 6:5 says the proposal "pleased the whole multitude").
- Full of faith and the Holy Spirit; the qualities the church required before any task.
- Wisdom in speaking that opponents could not answer (Acts 6:10).
- Deep, ordered knowledge of Israel's story and the ability to explain it (Acts 7:2–53).
- Courage before authorities and composure under false accusation (Acts 6:15).
- Forgiveness toward people doing him grave wrong (Acts 7:60).
- Combines practical service with teaching; Acts gives the Seven a serving task and then shows two of them, Stephen and Philip, speaking publicly.

## Development areas

Scripture records no failure by Stephen. Do not invent one. Development in 2026 is growth in a real responsibility, not correction of a biblical flaw.

- **Carrying a ministry that runs twice a week.** Data Play fiction: the load of Wednesday and Friday distribution, receiving and volunteers can crowd out prayer and Scripture, the very thing the apostles protected in Acts 6:4.
- **Delegating operational detail.** The Seven existed so that responsibility would be shared. Stephen should build a team rather than become the single person who knows how everything works.
- **Directness that can land hard.** Interpretation, handled with care: Stephen's speech ends with a sharp charge (Acts 7:51–53). It was prophetic confrontation before a council, not a model for volunteer feedback, and it should not be turned into a personality flaw. In 2026 fiction Stephen may occasionally be more direct than a volunteer expects when he sees someone overlooked, and he learns to pair truth with visible care.

## Stephen's death in 2026 records

Stephen is alive and serving in 2026 Data Play fiction. His stoning is nevertheless his lived history, and it is grave. Handle it this way:

1. **Routine records never mention it.** Weekly MTE reports, meeting notes, calendar entries, tasks, goals and comments do not refer to his death, directly or by allusion.
2. **No one jokes about it, and no one jokes about his being alive.** No banter, no "survivor" framing, no administrative humor about stones, cloaks, councils or witnesses. This applies to every character, including in comments.
3. **Rare, deliberate records only.** A testimony or a restricted personal reflection (`Only me`, or `Named people` naming one or two people) may touch it, written with restraint in Stephen's own voice. Focus on what Scripture records him seeing and saying: Jesus standing at God's right hand, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit!" and "Lord, don't hold this sin against them!" Do not describe the violence in detail.
4. **Do not narrate beyond the text.** Acts says "he fell asleep" (Acts 7:60). Do not invent what dying was like, what Stephen experienced afterward or any account of the afterlife.
5. **Events after his death are not his memory.** The burial, the lament, the scattering, the spread to Samaria and Antioch, and Saul's conversion happened after Stephen died. Stephen may know them as the church's history, never as things he saw.
6. **Anything involving Paul and Stephen's death** needs a deliberate decision by the lead author and is never a routine or shared record. See `relationships.md`.

## Sensitivity

- **Martyrdom** (Acts 7:54–8:1) is grave. See the rules above.
- **The widows' complaint** (Acts 6:1) was a real injustice to vulnerable women along language and cultural lines. Do not treat it as petty grumbling.
- **Stephen's speech and Jewish people.** Stephen spoke as a Jew to fellow Jews ("Brothers and fathers", "our father Abraham", Acts 7:2). His charge of resisting the Spirit follows the pattern of Israel's own prophets. Never use it to write contempt for Jewish people.
- **False witnesses and mob violence** are not material for lighthearted "incident review" framing.
- **Saul/Paul:** see relationships.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| One of the Seven chosen when Hellenist widows were neglected in the daily service; named first | Biblical fact (Acts 6:1–5) |
| The Seven were the first "deacons" | Traditional interpretation. Acts 6 does not use the title. It uses the language of service (6:1–2), but Acts does not connect the Seven to the later "servants" (often translated "deacons") of 1 Timothy 3:8–13. |
| Stephen personally managed food distribution | Inference from his appointment. Acts records the appointment "over this business" (6:3), not any distribution work by Stephen. |
| Stephen was a Hellenist (Greek-speaking Jew) | Reasonable inference from his Greek name and the complaint's context; not stated. All seven names are Greek; only Nicolaus is identified as a proselyte (6:5). |
| Performed wonders and signs | Biblical fact (Acts 6:8); what they were is not recorded |
| Saul was among the men from Cilicia who disputed with Stephen | Speculation. Acts names men "of Cilicia" (6:9) and Saul was from Tarsus in Cilicia, but it does not place Saul in the dispute. Do not use. |
| Details in Stephen's speech that differ from or add to the Old Testament accounts (e.g., Moses "instructed in all the wisdom of the Egyptians" and forty years old, 7:22–23; the Shechem tomb "that Abraham bought", 7:16, compare Genesis 23:17–19 and Joshua 24:32) | Stephen's speech as Acts records it. Harmonisation is debated. Data Play does not resolve it, and it does not alter the Moses files. |
| Meaning of Jesus "standing" rather than seated at God's right hand (7:55–56) | Interpretation (e.g., rising to welcome or to witness for Stephen). Not explained in the text. |
| Whether the stoning was a formal sentence or mob action | Debated. Acts describes a council hearing that ended with his hearers rushing at him and throwing him out of the city (7:57–58). |
| "The first Christian martyr" | Church description. Acts does not use the phrase; Paul later calls him "Stephen, your witness" (22:20). |
| Age, family, occupation, background | Not recorded. Do not invent. |
