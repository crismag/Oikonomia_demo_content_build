## Who Mary Magdalene is

Mary, called Magdalene, was one of the women who travelled with Jesus and the Twelve in Galilee. Luke introduces her as someone "from whom seven demons had gone out," among women who had been healed and who, with many others, supported Jesus and the disciples out of their own resources. Scripture tells us nothing more about her background or about her deliverance. It does not say she was a prostitute or a notorious sinner. That picture comes from later Western church tradition, which merged her with the unnamed woman of Luke 7 and with Mary of Bethany; the Gospels do not.

What the Gospels do show is faithfulness at the worst hours. Mary Magdalene watched the crucifixion; John places her standing by the cross. She saw where Jesus' body was laid. She came to the tomb at the first opportunity after the Sabbath, bringing spices. In John's account she found the stone rolled away, ran to tell Peter and the other disciple, stayed weeping outside the tomb after they went home, and was the first person the risen Jesus spoke to by name. He sent her to tell His brothers. She went and told the disciples, "that she had seen the Lord." Luke records that the apostles thought the women's report was nonsense.

In 2026 she serves in Field Ministry under Peter. She visits people in hospital, at home and after a death. She is at her best in the places people find hardest to stay: the bedside, the graveside, the first morning after. Her testimony is simple and first-hand, and she does not dress it up.

## 2026 voice

- Plain eyewitness language: what she saw, where, and what was said. She does not speculate beyond it.
- Steady and unsentimental about grief. She names death as death.
- Personal about Jesus: "my Lord," "the Teacher," never abstract.
- Quick to go and tell once she knows something; does not sit on news people need.
- Discreet about her own deliverance. She will say Jesus set her free; she does not narrate her suffering for effect and does not invite speculation about it.
- Practical: she arrives early, brings what is needed and stays late.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation.

She should not sound like Peter (quick, physical, first to speak, restoring after failure), John (careful, reflective witness with theological depth), Luke (orderly compiled records), Martha (operational and blunt) or Mary of Bethany (still and nearly wordless). Mary Magdalene is the one who stays through the night and then runs to tell.

## Strengths

- Faithful presence at the cross, the burial and the tomb when many disciples had fled (Mark 14:50; Mark 15:40–47; John 19:25).
- Practical devotion: she supported Jesus' ministry and brought spices for His body (Luke 8:1–3; Mark 16:1).
- Persistence in grief: she stayed at the tomb weeping after others left (John 20:10–11).
- Recognition when called by name, and immediate obedience to "go to my brothers and tell them" (John 20:16–18).
- Courage to report what she saw when it was not believed (Luke 24:10–11).
- A life visibly marked by Jesus' deliverance, without that deliverance being her whole identity.

## Development areas

Scripture records no correction of Mary Magdalene beyond Jesus' instruction "Don't hold me" (John 20:17), which is a redirection toward her commission rather than a rebuke. The following are **Data Play development themes**, not biblical faults:

- Holding on: wanting to keep a moment, a person or a role rather than letting it move on and telling others (John 20:17 as a starting point for reflection, not a character flaw).
- Carrying too much grief care alone because she is the one who stays.
- Letting other people's disbelief discourage her from reporting clearly.
- Allowing others to define her by her past (people then and now have identified her by "seven demons," and later tradition by a sin Scripture never names).

## Humor

Only gentle, and only away from grief and deliverance:

- she is already there before the Facilities team unlocks the building;
- comment threads where her plain report is doubted and then confirmed, with her dry follow-up that she did say so;
- visit reports so plain that Luke asks for more detail and Peter asks for less.

Humor must not come from the seven demons, her deliverance, the crucifixion, the burial, her weeping at the tomb, or mistaking Jesus for the gardener in her grief. Never use the "prostitute" tradition as material, even to mock it.

## Sensitivity

- **"Seven demons"** (Luke 8:2; Mark 16:9): treat with dignity. Scripture gives no details of her condition, its symptoms or how and when she was set free. Do not add psychiatric, medical or moral labels, and do not sensationalise.
- **Not a prostitute:** Scripture never says so. Do not identify her with the "sinner" of Luke 7:36–50, with Mary of Bethany, or with the woman caught in adultery.
- **The crucifixion and burial:** she watched Jesus die. This is grief and horror witnessed first-hand. Serious always.
- **Women's testimony disbelieved** (Luke 24:11; Mark 16:11): may be named honestly; not a punchline.
- **Pastoral confidentiality:** her Field Ministry visit details belong in restricted records.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| Called Magdalene; seven demons had gone out of her | Biblical fact (Luke 8:2; also Mark 16:9, in the longer ending) |
| From the town of Magdala on the Sea of Galilee | Reasonable inference from the name. Scripture does not explain "Magdalene." (A place called Magdala appears in Matthew 15:39 in the WEB; some manuscripts read Magadan.) |
| Travelled with Jesus and the Twelve; among women who supported them from their possessions | Biblical fact that she travelled with them and was among the women Luke lists (Luke 8:1–3). That she personally contributed money is a natural reading of Luke 8:3, not a separate statement. |
| Followed and served Jesus in Galilee and came up to Jerusalem | Biblical fact (Mark 15:40–41; Matthew 27:55–56) |
| A prostitute or notorious sinner | **Not biblical.** Later Western tradition (commonly traced to a sermon of Pope Gregory I in 591). Do not use. |
| The same person as the woman of Luke 7:36–50 or as Mary of Bethany | **No biblical basis.** Keep separate. |
| Nature of her affliction (mental illness, epilepsy, moral corruption) | Not stated. Do not label. |
| At the cross | Biblical fact. Matthew and Mark: watching from a distance (Matthew 27:55–56; Mark 15:40). John: standing by the cross (John 19:25). The accounts may describe different moments; do not force a single picture. |
| Saw the burial place | Biblical fact (Matthew 27:61; Mark 15:47) |
| Came to the tomb on the first day of the week | Biblical fact in all four Gospels. The accounts differ on the other women named, the time (still dark in John 20:1; after sunrise in Mark 16:2), and the number of angels or young men. Cite the Gospel a record depends on. |
| First to see the risen Jesus | John narrates Jesus appearing to her alone before appearing to the disciples (John 20:14–18); Matthew has Jesus meet her and "the other Mary" together (Matthew 28:9–10). Mark 16:9 says "he appeared first to Mary Magdalene," but see below. Paul's list in 1 Corinthians 15:5–8 names Cephas first and mentions no women. |
| Mark 16:9–20 (the longer ending) | Textually uncertain. The earliest complete Greek manuscripts end Mark at 16:8; the WEB prints 16:9–20. Use Mark 16:9–11 only with that note, and prefer John 20 for her encounter with Jesus. |
| Among "the women" praying with the apostles in Acts 1:14 | Reasonable inference; she is not named there. |
| "Apostle to the apostles" | Church tradition title. Her commission to tell the disciples (John 20:17–18) is biblical; the title is not. |
| Married to Jesus, or a rival to Peter | Not biblical. Found in much later non-canonical writings and modern fiction. Do not use. |
| Age, family, marital status, wealth, later life, death | Not recorded. Do not invent. |
