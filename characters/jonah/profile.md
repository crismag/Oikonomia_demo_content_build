## Who Jonah is

Jonah son of Amittai was a prophet from Gath Hepher in the northern kingdom of Israel. 2 Kings 14:25 records that he prophesied the restoration of Israel's borders under King Jeroboam II, a king who "did that which was evil in the LORD's sight," because God saw how bitter Israel's suffering was.

The book of Jonah tells a different kind of assignment. God told Jonah to go to Nineveh, "that great city," capital of Assyria, the empire that threatened Israel, and preach against its wickedness. Jonah went the opposite way, paying his fare on a ship from Joppa to Tarshish. God sent a storm. The pagan sailors prayed, cast lots, and learned that Jonah was fleeing from the LORD. Jonah told them to throw him overboard; after trying to row to shore, they did, and the sea went calm. God appointed a huge fish to swallow Jonah. From inside it he prayed a psalm of distress and thanksgiving, and the fish vomited him onto land.

God spoke to him a second time, and he went. He walked a day into the city and announced, "In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!" The people believed God and fasted in sackcloth, from the greatest to the least; the king joined them and ordered everyone to turn from violence. God relented. Jonah was furious. He told God that this was exactly why he had fled: "I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness, and you relent of doing harm." He asked to die. God asked, "Is it right for you to be angry?" Jonah sat outside the city to watch. God grew a vine for shade, then sent a worm to kill it and a hot east wind, and Jonah again asked to die. God asked whether he was right to be angry about the vine, and the book ends with God's question: shouldn't He care about Nineveh, with more than 120,000 people who cannot tell their right hand from their left, and many animals? Jonah's answer is not recorded.

Jonah is not a joke about a fish. He is a capable prophet who knew God's character accurately and did not want it applied to people he considered enemies. His problem was not ignorance; it was his heart. The book leaves him, and the reader, with a question about compassion.

In 2026 he serves in Field Ministry under Peter, assigned to outreach and offsite services. He is articulate, theologically sharp and honest to the point of bluntness about what he resents. Peter is patient with him. God's question is still open.

## 2026 voice

- Articulate, quick and precise. He knows the right theology and will quote God's character back at you.
- Candid about resentment. He will say out loud what others only think.
- Dry and sardonic, sometimes sulky. Reports can be minimal when he disapproves of an assignment.
- Brief when he doesn't want to be there: his whole recorded message to Nineveh is one sentence.
- Can be moving when he talks about prayer from the bottom of the sea.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation. Not a clown.

He should not sound like Peter (warm, quick to act and volunteer), Elijah (terse but fully committed), Jeremiah (grieving for the people he warns) or Elisha (patient, practical compassion). Jonah is the one who can explain God's mercy perfectly and still not want it to reach certain people.

## Strengths

- Knows God's character accurately, in the words of Israel's confession (Jonah 4:2; compare Exodus 34:6).
- Honest: he told the sailors who he was and what he had done (1:9–10), and told God exactly why he fled (4:2).
- Took responsibility for the storm and told the sailors to throw him into the sea rather than let them die for him (1:12).
- Prayed in extremity and gave thanks before he was rescued (2:1–9).
- Obeyed the second assignment, and the city responded to his message (3:3–5).
- A genuine prophetic ministry in Israel as well (2 Kings 14:25).

## Development areas

- **Running from an assignment:** he fled toward Tarshish, "from the presence of the LORD" (1:3).
- **Sleeping through a crisis his choices caused** while others prayed and threw cargo overboard (1:5–6).
- **Anger at mercy for people he did not want helped** (4:1–2).
- **Valuing his own comfort over people:** he grieved the vine more than he cared for Nineveh (4:9–11).
- **Asking to die when things did not go his way** (4:3, 8). This is serious, not comic.
- **Watching for judgment instead of rejoicing in repentance:** he sat east of the city to see what would happen (4:5).

Failure is not his identity, and the book does not finish his story. Records must not invent a resolution Scripture does not give.

## Core relationships

Jonah's biblical relationships are few; they are summarised here instead of in a separate file.

### Biblical
- **The LORD:** who called him twice, pursued him through a storm and a fish, answered his prayer, spared Nineveh, and questioned his anger gently and persistently. The book is about this relationship.
- **The sailors** (not on the roster): they prayed to their gods, woke him, tried to save him, prayed to the LORD not to hold his death against them, and ended by fearing the LORD, sacrificing and making vows (1:5–16). They showed more compassion for Jonah than he showed for Nineveh.
- **The king and people of Nineveh** (not on the roster): believed God, fasted, wore sackcloth and turned from violence (3:5–9). The king is not named.
- **Jeroboam II** (not on the roster): the evil king under whom Jonah's word of restored borders was fulfilled (2 Kings 14:23–27).

### 2026 fiction
- **Peter — reporting leader.** Peter heads Field Ministry and gives Jonah assignments. Peter knows what it is to run and be sent again (the denial and John 21). Peter also once had to cross a deep boundary to go to Gentiles, from a rooftop in Joppa, the same port Jonah sailed from (Acts 10). The Joppa link is a reader's observation, not a biblical connection; neither remembers the other's story. **Namesake:** Peter's father is called Jonah in John 1:42 and 21:15–17 (WEB); that man is not the prophet. Any joke about it must say so plainly.
- **Elisha — Field Ministry teammate.** Elisha healed Naaman, a commander of an enemy army. The contrast with Jonah's resentment of Nineveh is a serious coaching conversation. Jewish tradition identifies Jonah as the young prophet Elisha sent to anoint Jehu; this is not Scripture and must not be presented as fact.
- **Isaiah and Jeremiah** — fellow prophets. Isaiah's later era saw Assyria devastate Judah and Sennacherib return to Nineveh (Isaiah 37:37). Jonah has no memory of that, but it shows what Assyria meant to Israel and gives weight to Jonah's hostility without excusing it.
- **Elijah** — who also sat down and asked to die (1 Kings 19:4). A restricted peer conversation is plausible.
- **Cornelius and Philip the Evangelist** — whose welcome of outsiders gives Jonah's development a 2026 contrast. Jonah's work with them is fiction.
- **Cris** — who processes Jonah's travel-deviation and expense questions and cannot see his restricted development records unless named.

## Humor

Humor comes from reluctance, reassignment and administrative framing, never from danger, suffering or repentance:

- **Travel deviation:** assigned to Nineveh, booked Tarshish, and "paid its fare" himself (1:3). Expense claim questions from Cris.
- **Second assignment:** "The LORD's word came to Jonah the second time" (3:1). A reassignment notice with the same destination.
- **Shortest sermon:** a one-sentence message to a city of three days' journey (3:3–4). Outreach report with a very short "What was said" section.
- **Result he didn't want:** an outreach report that records a huge positive response under "Concerns."
- **The booth and the vine:** Facilities and shade requests east of the city.
- **Sleeping below deck** while the crew bails (1:5). Only as reluctance, never the danger.

Humor must not come from the storm's danger, the sailors' terror, Jonah being thrown overboard, his near-drowning and prayer, Nineveh's violence and repentance, God's compassion for 120,000 people and their animals, or Jonah's requests to die.

## Sensitivity

- **Requests to die** (4:3, 8): Scripture records anger, exhaustion from heat and a stated preference for death. Do not attach a clinical label. God's response was a question, not a joke.
- **Storm and sea** (1:4–2:10): terror, near-death, and a prayer from "the belly of Sheol."
- **Nineveh:** Assyria was historically violent toward Israel; the king himself speaks of "the violence that is in his hands" (3:8). Jonah's hostility has a real background. It is not treated as merely petty, and it is not excused. Never use it to caricature any modern nation or people.
- **God's compassion and judgment:** Nineveh's repentance and God relenting are theologically serious (3:10). Do not trivialise either.
- **Ethnic and national hatred:** must never become comedy.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| Son of Amittai, from Gath Hepher; prophesied Israel's restored borders under Jeroboam II | Biblical fact (Jonah 1:1; 2 Kings 14:25). |
| Family, marriage, age, occupation beyond prophet | Not recorded. Do not invent them. |
| Why Jonah fled | Jonah states his own reason: he knew God is gracious and would relent (4:2). That Assyria's cruelty or national loyalty also motivated him is a reasonable inference, not stated. |
| "Huge fish" versus "whale" | The WEB reads "huge fish" (1:17; Matthew 12:40). "Whale" comes from older English translations of Matthew. The species is not specified. |
| Three days and three nights | Biblical fact (1:17). Jesus used it as a sign of His death and resurrection (Matthew 12:40). |
| Chapter and verse numbering | In the WEB and most English Bibles the fish appears at 1:17 and the prayer begins at 2:1; Hebrew Bibles number these 2:1–2:2. |
| Jonah's message was only the sentence recorded | The text records one sentence (3:4), five words in Hebrew. Whether he said more is not stated. |
| Nineveh "three days' journey across" and "more than one hundred twenty thousand persons" | Biblical fact (3:3; 4:11). How these figures relate to archaeology is debated. |
| "Can't discern between their right hand and their left hand" | Biblical phrase (4:11). Interpreted as children, as moral ignorance, or as the whole population; do not settle it. |
| Jonah's response to God's final question | Not recorded. **Do not invent it.** |
| Historicity and genre of the book | Debated: read as historical narrative, as a parable-like prophetic narrative, or as both. Jesus referred to Jonah and the men of Nineveh (Matthew 12:39–41; Luke 11:29–32). Data Play treats Jonah's account as his lived history without arguing the question. |
| Jonah was the young prophet who anointed Jehu, or the widow of Zarephath's son | Jewish traditions. Not Scripture. |
| Jonah's tomb or later life | Tradition only. Not recorded in Scripture. |
| Nineveh's later fall | Prophesied in Nahum. After Jonah's recorded life; Jonah has no memory of it. |
| Peter's father "Jonah" | A different person (John 1:42; 21:15–17). Some manuscripts read "John." |
