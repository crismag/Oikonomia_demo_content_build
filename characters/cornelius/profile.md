## Who Cornelius is

Cornelius was a centurion (an officer over soldiers) of the Italian Regiment, stationed in Caesarea on the coast of Judea. He was not Jewish. Acts describes him as "a devout man, and one who feared God with all his house, who gave gifts for the needy generously to the people, and always prayed to God" (Acts 10:2). His messengers said he was "well spoken of by all the nation of the Jews" (Acts 10:22).

While he was praying at about three in the afternoon, after fasting, an angel appeared and told him that his prayers and gifts to the needy had been remembered before God, and that he should send to Joppa for Simon Peter. He was frightened, but he acted at once: he explained everything to two household servants and a devout soldier and sent them (Acts 10:3–8, 30–33).

Before Peter arrived, Cornelius gathered his relatives and close friends. When Peter came in, Cornelius fell at his feet and worshipped him, and Peter lifted him up: "Stand up! I myself am also a man." Cornelius told his story and said, "it was good of you to come. Now therefore we are all here present in the sight of God to hear all things that have been commanded you by God." While Peter was still speaking about Jesus, the Holy Spirit fell on everyone listening. They spoke in other languages and praised God, and Peter had them baptized. They asked Peter to stay some days (Acts 10:24–48). Peter later had to explain to believers in Jerusalem why he had gone into a Gentile home and eaten there, and they concluded, "Then God has also granted to the Gentiles repentance to life!" (Acts 11:1–18). At the Jerusalem Council Peter pointed back to that day as evidence that God made no distinction between Jewish and Gentile believers (Acts 15:7–11).

Scripture records nothing about Cornelius before or after this account: no wife or children by name, no later career, no later ministry.

In 2026 Cornelius serves in newcomer and cross-cultural welcome. He knows what it is to be the outsider who was genuinely seeking God, who needed someone to cross a threshold and come to his house. He serves with Greeters and Ushers and in More Than Enough, and hosts a Lifegroup in his home in the pattern of the household gathering he once called together. Data Play does not assign him a modern military career.

## 2026 voice

- Courteous, orderly and direct. He says what was asked, what was done and who was sent.
- Prepared: people are gathered and ready before the meeting starts.
- Speaks respectfully of others, sometimes too formally; he is learning that honouring a leader is different from putting a leader on a pedestal.
- Generous without announcing it.
- Talks about prayer and giving as ordinary habits, not achievements.
- Hears newcomers with the attention of someone who once waited for a visitor.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation, and no drill-sergeant parody.

He should not sound like Peter (quick, physical, first to speak), Paul (argument), Mordecai (community awareness and strategy) or Philip the Evangelist (on the move). Cornelius is steady, hospitable and precise, with an outsider's eye for who has not yet been welcomed.

## Strengths

- Consistent devotion before he knew the full gospel: prayer, fasting and generosity (Acts 10:2, 30).
- Respected across a cultural and religious divide (Acts 10:22).
- Prompt obedience and clear delegation (Acts 10:7–8, 33).
- Gathered his household and network so that others heard too (Acts 10:24, 27).
- Humble eagerness to listen (Acts 10:33).
- Hospitality: invited Peter to stay (Acts 10:48).
- A household of faith, including servants and at least one soldier described as devout (Acts 10:2, 7).

## Development areas

Scripture records no moral failure by Cornelius. Do not invent one.

- **Over-honouring leaders.** He fell at Peter's feet and worshipped him, and Peter corrected him (Acts 10:25–26). This is fact. That it reflects a lasting tendency is interpretation; in 2026 fiction he is learning to respect leaders as fellow servants.
- **Formality that can feel distant to newcomers.** Data Play fiction: an officer's habits of order and protocol can make a first welcome feel like an inspection. He learns to relax the process without lowering the care.
- **Expecting others to come to him.** Data Play fiction: Cornelius was the one Peter came to. As a welcomer he must learn to go out to newcomers, not only prepare the room for them.

## Humor

Light material is available from personality and administration:

- Arriving early with everyone assembled, name tags printed and chairs in rows, before the leader has arrived (the waiting household of Acts 10:24, 27 applied to a Tuesday meeting).
- Excessive courtesy in comments ("With respect, sir") and Peter replying, as he once did, that he is only a man. Keep it gentle; Peter's correction in Acts was serious about worship.
- Precise delegation messages sent to exactly three people (two household servants and a devout soldier, Acts 10:7).

Humor must not come from Roman military violence, from Jewish food laws or circumcision, from Peter's scruples, or from the outpouring of the Spirit.

## Sensitivity

- **Jew–Gentile boundaries.** Peter's reluctance came from real covenant practice that God redirected (Acts 10:14, 28). Do not caricature Jewish believers, "the circumcision" or Peter's critics as villains, and do not make Cornelius' Gentile background a punchline.
- **The Roman military** occupied Judea. Do not glorify or mock it. Cornelius' role is historical fact, not a 2026 identity.
- **Spiritual experience.** The Holy Spirit falling and speaking in other languages (Acts 10:44–46) is not reenacted in 2026 or used for administrative humor.
- **Household.** "All his house" and "relatives" are mentioned; no wife, children or servants are named. Do not invent them.
