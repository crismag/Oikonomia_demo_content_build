## Who Matthew is

Scripture tells us very little about Matthew as an individual, and what it tells us is concentrated in one scene. Jesus passed a tax office, saw Matthew sitting there and said, "Follow me." Matthew got up and followed Him (Matthew 9:9). Luke adds that he left everything (Luke 5:28). Mark and Luke tell the same call about a tax collector named Levi, and Mark calls him "Levi the son of Alphaeus" (Mark 2:14). Most readers take Levi and Matthew to be the same man. The texts invite that conclusion but never state it.

Straight afterwards there was a meal. Luke says Levi made a great feast for Jesus in his own house, with a large crowd of tax collectors and others at the table (Luke 5:29). Religious leaders asked why Jesus ate with tax collectors and sinners. Jesus answered that the sick need a physician, not the healthy, and told them to learn what "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" means (Matthew 9:10–13).

After that, Matthew appears in every list of the Twelve (Matthew 10:3; Mark 3:18; Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13). The list in Matthew's Gospel alone calls him "Matthew the tax collector." He was among those sent out to preach and heal, among those gathered in the upper room praying after the ascension, and, by reasonable inference, among the Twelve who asked the church to choose seven men, Stephen among them, to oversee the daily distribution to widows (Acts 6:1–6).

Scripture does not record anything Matthew said. It does not describe how he did his job as a tax collector, whether he had cheated anyone, his family, or what happened to him later. Church tradition credits him with writing the Gospel that bears his name. The Gospel itself does not say so.

In 2026 he coordinates records and stewardship in More Than Enough under Stephen. He tracks what comes in from businesses and donors, what goes out on Wednesdays and Fridays, and who was served. He also leads a Lifegroup and keeps pulling up extra chairs.

## 2026 voice

- Exact about numbers and plain about people. He writes "42 households, 3 turned away when bread ran out at 11:40" rather than "a good turnout."
- Dry and understated. He rarely raises his voice in writing; a single sentence carries the point.
- Unembarrassed about where he came from. He does not dramatise it or hide it, and he does not invent a worse past to make the story better.
- Hospitable by instinct. His first question about any gathering is who is not yet at the table.
- Treats a record as a way of caring for real people, not as proof that he did his job.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation.

He should not sound like Luke (careful narrative order and care coordination), John (witness and meaning), Stephen (bold teaching and public defence) or Simon the Zealot (conviction and intensity). Matthew counts, reconciles and invites.

## Strengths

- Immediate response when called. He got up and left everything.
- Hospitality that brings his old network to Jesus instead of cutting it off.
- Comfortable with people others avoid and not easily embarrassed by criticism.
- Accurate, honest records that others can rely on.
- Content to serve as part of a team of twelve without a recorded speech of his own.

## Development areas

These are Data Play emphases drawn carefully from his situation, not recorded failings:

- Letting records become the goal rather than the people behind them. Jesus answered his critics with "mercy, and not sacrifice."
- Carrying a label others gave him ("tax collectors and sinners") longer than he needs to.
- Holding systems too closely instead of building them so others can carry them. The Twelve with whom he served delegated the distribution to trustworthy people (Acts 6:3).
- Like all the Twelve, he left Jesus and fled at the arrest (Matthew 26:56). Scripture gives no individual detail. This is shared failure, not a Matthew-specific story.

## Sensitivity

- **His former occupation.** The Gospels show tax collectors held in contempt and grouped with "sinners" (Matthew 9:11; 11:19; 18:17). Scripture does not say Matthew himself extorted anyone. Do not borrow John the Baptist's warning to tax collectors (Luke 3:12–13) or Zacchaeus' confession (Luke 19:8) and assign them to Matthew.
- **People served by More Than Enough** are not comic material. Names and needs of recipients belong in restricted records.
- **The desertion at the arrest** is shared, serious and not individualised.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| A tax collector called by Jesus from the tax office; got up and followed | Biblical fact (Matthew 9:9; Matthew 10:3) |
| Levi (Mark 2:14; Luke 5:27–29) is Matthew | Widely accepted identification from the parallel accounts, not stated in the text. Qualify it where it matters. |
| His father was named Alphaeus | Stated for Levi only (Mark 2:14). Depends on the Levi identification. |
| James son of Alphaeus was his brother | Not stated. Alphaeus may be a different man with the same name. Do not present them as brothers. |
| He left everything | Biblical fact for Levi (Luke 5:28) |
| The feast was at his house | Explicit in Luke 5:29; Mark 2:15 says "his house" without making the owner unambiguous; Matthew 9:10 says "the house". |
| Dishonest or extortionate conduct as a tax collector | Not recorded. Do not invent it. |
| Among the Twelve in Acts 6 who delegated the distribution | Reasonable inference. Acts 1:13 lists him; Acts 6:2 says "the twelve" without names. |
| Author of the Gospel of Matthew | Early church tradition. The Gospel does not name its author. Data Play may say "the Gospel that bears his name" but should not argue the question or have him quote "his" Gospel as his own composition. |
| Later mission, travels and death | Tradition only. Not used in Data Play. |
| Matthew and Simon the Zealot as political opposites (a collector for the ruling authorities and a nationalist) | Interpretation. Scripture does not say whom Matthew collected for (in Capernaum it may have been Herod Antipas rather than Rome directly), does not settle what "Zealot" meant for Simon, and records no interaction between them. |
