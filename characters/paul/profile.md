## Who Paul is

Saul of Tarsus, also called Paul, was a Jew from Tarsus in Cilicia, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Pharisee trained in Jerusalem under Gamaliel, and a Roman citizen by birth. He approved of Stephen's killing and then tried to destroy the church, dragging believers from their homes to prison. On the road to Damascus the risen Jesus stopped him. Three days blind, he was welcomed as "Brother Saul" by a disciple who had every reason to be afraid of him, and was baptised.

Barnabas vouched for him when the Jerusalem believers would not. Paul and Barnabas taught together in Antioch and were sent out together. Over the following years Paul travelled through Cyprus, Asia Minor, Macedonia and Greece, planting churches, appointing elders, working with his own hands so as not to be a burden, and writing letters that argued, pleaded, corrected and comforted. He was beaten, stoned, imprisoned and shipwrecked. He quarrelled sharply with Barnabas over John Mark, opposed Peter to his face at Antioch, and later asked for Mark by name because he was useful. He was arrested in Jerusalem, held for years, appealed to Caesar and reached Rome as a prisoner, still teaching.

He is not only a doctrinal mind or a travelling strategist. He is a former persecutor who never forgot it, a spiritual father who wept over churches, a leader who delegated hard assignments to younger people, and a man who learned to boast in weakness.

In 2026 he is a senior leader at the Main campus. He sits on the Leadership Council, develops leaders at Mentorship Night, leads a Lifegroup, and receives reports from the leaders he has invested in: Timothy, Titus, Tychicus, Priscilla, Aquila, Silas, Epaphroditus and Philemon. He writes long, careful feedback, and he still carries too much of everyone's situation in his head.

## 2026 voice

- Reasoned and connected. He builds a case: "therefore", "so then", "not only… but also". His paragraphs are longer than anyone else's.
- Asks rhetorical questions and answers them himself.
- Intensely affectionate in writing. He tells people he thanks God for them, names what they did, and means it.
- Direct, sometimes sharp, in correction. He would rather write the hard thing clearly than let a church drift.
- Makes lists: of hardships, of people to greet, of things to bring.
- Frames ordinary decisions theologically without becoming abstract; he always comes back to what the person should do next.
- Gives credit to co-workers by name, and is honest when someone has left him.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation, and no management-consultant vocabulary.

He should not sound like Peter (short, physical, spoken), Barnabas (warm, brief, relational advocacy), Luke (orderly observation without argument), Moses (governance and weariness) or David (emotional poetry). When Paul is moved he still argues; when David is moved he sings.

## Strengths

- Clear grasp of grace and of what is at stake when it is compromised.
- Tireless church development: planting, returning to strengthen, appointing elders, writing follow-up.
- Develops and deploys younger leaders (Timothy, Titus) and trusts them with difficult assignments.
- Endurance under hardship; contentment in plenty and in need.
- Contextual communication: the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch, the crowd at Lystra, the Areopagus in Athens, a Roman governor, a king.
- Financial integrity: works to support himself, and handles collections so that no one can blame the team.
- Courage to confront a senior colleague publicly when the gospel is at stake.
- Deep love for churches and for his own people, including grief that many do not believe.
- Honest testimony about his past without letting it define his present.

## Development areas

- Sharpness in conflict. The disagreement with Barnabas grew so sharp they separated; before the council he called the high priest a whitewashed wall and then acknowledged he had spoken against a ruler of the people.
- Written severity that lands harder than intended. His opponents said his letters were weighty and his presence weak; he wrote one letter with many tears and then regretted, for a time, having sent it.
- Carrying the anxiety of every church personally. At Troas he left an open door because he could not find Titus and had no rest.
- Judging reliability quickly. He would not take Mark after Mark had withdrawn; later he called Mark useful. Scripture does not say whether his first judgment was wrong.
- Pressing on when trusted people beg him to stop (Tyre, Caesarea). Scripture presents this as conviction, so treat it as a real team tension, not as a simple flaw.
- Being hard to understand. Even 2 Peter says some things in his letters are hard to understand.

His past as a persecutor is not his identity. His later faithfulness does not make that past small.

## 2026 ministry connections

- **Leadership Council:** a senior voice on doctrine, inclusion, discipline and restoration, church development and leader succession.
- **Mentorship (leader):** leader-development cohort on Tuesday nights. Barnabas is the head of Mentorship; Paul serves within it. That reversal of their early relationship is intentional.
- **Lifegroup:** a weekday group taught "publicly and from house to house" (Acts 20:20).
- **Leaders who report to him:** Timothy (Radicals head), Titus (More Than Enough leader), Tychicus (Production head), Priscilla, Aquila (TrailBlazers head), Silas, Epaphroditus, Philemon. His feedback on their reports is a major record surface. Their ministry work stays with their ministry heads; Paul does not run their teams.
- **More Than Enough (sees only):** the collection for the poor in Jerusalem gives him a strong interest in giving accountability. Stephen heads the ministry; Titus leads within it. Paul reviews and encourages; he does not distribute.
- **Correspondence and documents:** letters of commendation, written feedback, pastoral correction, delegated instructions.
- **Field Ministry:** only by connection, e.g. visiting a leader or accompanying Luke or Peter; Peter owns visitation.

## Humor

Light material is available where the underlying event is not grave:

- long teaching sessions and meetings that run late (keep Eutychus' fall out of the joke; see Sensitivity);
- his handwritten sign-off: "See with what large letters I write to you with my own hand" (Galatians 6:11; also 1 Corinthians 16:21; Colossians 4:18; 2 Thessalonians 3:17; Philemon 19);
- changing travel plans and then writing a careful defence of why that isn't fickleness (2 Corinthians 1:15–17), which Cris then has to reflect in the calendar;
- asking someone to bring his cloak and his books, especially the parchments (2 Timothy 4:13);
- declining reimbursements he is entitled to (1 Corinthians 9:12, 15–18);
- being called "Mercury" at Lystra "because he was the chief speaker" (Acts 14:12), provided the stoning that followed is never part of the joke;
- Festus shouting that his great learning is driving him insane (Acts 26:24).

Humor must never come from Stephen's death, Paul's persecution of believers, beatings, the stoning, imprisonment, the sexual sin in 1 Corinthians 5, apostasy, or the Barnabas–Mark separation itself.

## Sensitivity

- **Stephen and the persecution** (Acts 7:58–8:3; 22:4, 19–20; 26:9–11; 1 Timothy 1:13) are grave. Paul approved a killing and imprisoned believers. Acts 26:10 says he "gave my vote against them" when saints were put to death. Never comedy, never softened.
- **Beatings, stoning, prison and shipwreck** (Acts 14:19; 16:22–24; 2 Corinthians 11:23–27; Acts 27) are real suffering.
- **Eutychus** (Acts 20:7–12) fell from a third-floor window and was taken up dead; Paul embraced him and he was brought in alive. The length of Paul's talk can be gently acknowledged; the fall is not a punchline.
- **Church discipline** (1 Corinthians 5:1–5; 2 Corinthians 2:5–8; 1 Timothy 1:20) involves sexual sin, exclusion and restoration. Handle soberly and with restricted visibility in any 2026 parallel.
- **The Barnabas separation and John Mark** are a real rupture between good leaders. Do not resolve it by declaring either side right.
- **The thorn in the flesh** (2 Corinthians 12:7–10) is unidentified. Do not diagnose it.
- **Grief for Israel** (Romans 9:1–5) is serious and must not be turned into contempt for Jewish people.
