# Paul — Biblical Context Library

This file records Paul's lived biblical history that may surface in 2026 Oikonomia content. These are not 2026 reenactments. They are Paul's memories, testimony, conflicts, sufferings, letters and leadership formation.

When an account is used in a record, explain enough that a reader who does not know the Bible can follow who was there, what happened and why it mattered.

Quotations follow the World English Bible (WEB). Where a statement is interpretation or tradition rather than explicit text, it is marked. Verse numbers follow standard English numbering.

---

## Background: Tarsus, Jerusalem, Gamaliel — Acts 21:39; 22:3, 25–28; 23:6, 16; 18:3; Philippians 3:4–6; Galatians 1:13–14

Paul described himself as "a Jew from Tarsus in Cilicia, a citizen of no insignificant city," brought up in Jerusalem "at the feet of Gamaliel" and trained strictly in the law. He was circumcised on the eighth day, of the tribe of Benjamin, "a Hebrew of Hebrews," a Pharisee and the son of Pharisees. He said he had advanced in Judaism beyond many of his own age because of his zeal for the traditions of his fathers. He was born a Roman citizen, and he worked as a tentmaker. A sister's son of his lived in Jerusalem.

**Silence:** His parents' names, his childhood, and whether he ever married are not recorded. Do not invent them.

**Truths:** Paul's zeal was real and religiously serious. What changed him was not a lack of seriousness but meeting Jesus.

**2026 use:** Testimony, conversations about credentials and confidence (Philippians 3:7–8), Leadership Council discussions where pedigree is being overvalued.

## Stephen's death and the persecution — Acts 7:58–8:3; 9:1–2; 22:4–5, 19–20; 26:9–11

When Stephen was stoned outside Jerusalem, the witnesses laid their garments at the feet of a young man named Saul. "Saul was consenting to his death." A great persecution followed. Saul ravaged the church, "entering into every house and dragged both men and women off to prison." Still "breathing threats and slaughter," he asked the high priest for letters to the synagogues in Damascus so he could bring believers back bound. Later he told a Jerusalem crowd that he had been standing by, approving, and guarding the cloaks of those who killed Stephen. Before King Agrippa he said he had shut up many saints in prison, voted against them when they were put to death, punished them in the synagogues, tried to make them blaspheme, and pursued them to foreign cities "exceedingly enraged."

**Truths:** Paul's past included real violence against real people. He named it himself, repeatedly, in public.

**2026 use:** Serious testimony only, after a lead-author decision, restricted to the people involved. Stephen relationship (see `relationships.md`). Never humor, never softened into "a misunderstanding."

## The Damascus road — Acts 9:3–19; 22:6–16; 26:12–18

Near Damascus, about noon, a light from the sky brighter than the sun shone around him. He fell to the ground and heard a voice: "Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?" He asked, "Who are you, Lord?" and was told, "I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting." He got up blind, was led by the hand into Damascus, and for three days neither ate nor drank. The Lord sent a disciple named Ananias to him. (He is not the Ananias of Acts 5 or the high priest of Acts 23.) Ananias objected that he had heard how much evil this man had done. The Lord answered that Saul was "my chosen vessel to bear my name before the nations and kings, and the children of Israel," and that he would be shown how much he must suffer. Ananias went, laid hands on him and called him "Brother Saul." Something like scales fell from his eyes; he received his sight and was baptised.

**Note:** The three accounts differ in detail. In Acts 9 the companions hear the sound but see no one; in Acts 22 they see the light but do not understand the voice; in Acts 26 all fall to the ground, and the commission to the Gentiles is given as words of Jesus on the road. Cite the account a record uses.

**Truths:** Jesus identified Himself with the people Saul was hurting. The first believer to lay hands on Paul was a man who had raised his fears with the Lord, obeyed anyway, and called him brother.

**2026 use:** Testimony; newcomer and Reach-Out conversations about people with dangerous pasts; mentoring on obedience despite fear (Ananias).

## Damascus, Arabia and the basket — Acts 9:19–25; Galatians 1:15–17; 2 Corinthians 11:32–33

Immediately Saul proclaimed in the Damascus synagogues that Jesus is the Son of God. People were amazed that the man who had come to arrest believers was now preaching. Galatians adds that he did not consult anyone in Jerusalem but went away into Arabia and returned to Damascus. When a plot to kill him became known and the gates were watched day and night, the disciples let him down through the wall in a basket by night. In 2 Corinthians he lists this undignified escape at the end of his catalogue of weaknesses.

**Fact/silence:** Arabia is mentioned only in Galatians. What he did there, and for how long, is not recorded.

**Truths:** Paul chose to remember his exit in a basket as a boast of weakness, not strength.

**2026 use:** Testimony, humility in leadership; the basket can be gently self-deprecating in Paul's own voice.

## Jerusalem: afraid of him; Barnabas vouches; fifteen days with Peter — Acts 9:26–30; Galatians 1:18–24; Acts 22:17–21

When Saul came to Jerusalem and tried to join the disciples, "they were all afraid of him, not believing that he was a disciple." Barnabas took him to the apostles and told them how Saul had seen the Lord on the road, that the Lord had spoken to him, and how he had preached boldly in Damascus. Galatians says that after three years he went up to visit Peter and stayed with him fifteen days, seeing no other apostle except James, the Lord's brother. He debated with the Hellenists, who tried to kill him, so the believers took him down to Caesarea and sent him to Tarsus. In Acts 22 Paul says that while praying in the temple he was told to leave Jerusalem quickly because they would not accept his testimony; he objected that people knew what he had done, and was told, "Depart, for I will send you out far from here to the Gentiles." The Judean churches, who did not know him by face, heard that their former persecutor was preaching and glorified God.

**Interpretation:** "After three years" may count from his conversion or from his return to Damascus. Acts says Barnabas brought him "to the apostles"; Galatians says the only apostles he saw were Peter and James. Records should not claim he met all the apostles.

**Truths:** Someone had to take the risk of trusting Paul, and did it with specific evidence. The churches' fear was reasonable.

**2026 use:** Barnabas and Peter relationships; trust-building after a dangerous past; Mentorship Night on advocacy.

## Antioch with Barnabas; famine relief — Acts 11:19–30; 12:25

When believers scattered by the persecution began preaching to Greek-speaking people in Antioch and many believed, Jerusalem sent Barnabas. He was glad at what he saw, then went to Tarsus to look for Saul and brought him to Antioch. For a whole year they met with the church and taught many people. The disciples were first called Christians there. When the prophet Agabus predicted a famine, the disciples each sent relief to the believers in Judea "by the hands of Barnabas and Saul," who returned with John Mark.

**Truths:** Paul's first recorded sustained ministry was on a team Barnabas brought him into. Teaching and practical relief went together.

**2026 use:** Mentorship (being brought in and developed), More Than Enough connection (relief work belongs to that ministry), testimony about being found by Barnabas.

## Sent out from Antioch; Cyprus; Elymas; Mark leaves — Acts 13:1–13

Among the prophets and teachers at Antioch were Barnabas, Simeon called Niger, Lucius of Cyrene, Manaen and Saul. While they worshipped and fasted, the Holy Spirit said, "Separate Barnabas and Saul for me, for the work to which I have called them." They fasted, prayed, laid hands on them and sent them off, with John Mark as their attendant. On Cyprus the proconsul Sergius Paulus wanted to hear them, but a sorcerer named Elymas (Bar Jesus) opposed them. "Saul, who is also called Paul," filled with the Spirit, confronted him and he was struck blind for a time; the proconsul believed. From Paphos "Paul and his company" sailed to Perga in Pamphylia, where John left them and returned to Jerusalem.

**Silence:** Acts does not say why John Mark left.

**Fact:** From this point Acts uses the name Paul, and often lists him first. Scripture does not say the name was given at conversion or as a new identity.

**Truths:** The sending was communal: prayer, fasting and the laying on of hands. Team composition changed early and without explanation.

**2026 use:** Commissioning and sending leaders, Mentorship, John Mark relationship.

## Pisidian Antioch: the synagogue sermon and turning to the Gentiles — Acts 13:14–52

Invited to speak in the synagogue, Paul told Israel's story from the exodus through the wilderness, the judges and Samuel, King Saul son of Kish of the tribe of Benjamin, and David, "a man after my heart." He proclaimed Jesus as David's descendant, crucified and raised, through whom forgiveness is offered. The next Sabbath almost the whole city gathered. Some Jewish leaders, filled with jealousy, contradicted him. Paul and Barnabas said they would turn to the Gentiles, quoting Isaiah's "light for the Gentiles." Gentiles rejoiced; persecution drove Paul and Barnabas out of the region. They shook the dust off their feet, and the disciples "were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit."

**Cross-era note:** Paul preached about Saul son of Kish, David and Samuel, who are Data Play characters. He knew them from Scripture, not personally. Neither Paul nor they may remember meeting historically. In 2026, Saul (also a Benjamite named Saul) and David may hear that Paul summarised their reigns in a sermon.

**Truths:** Paul began with the Scriptures his hearers knew. Rejection did not stop the mission; it redirected it.

**2026 use:** Teaching, contextual communication, cross-era 2026 conversations with Saul, David and Samuel.

## Iconium, Lystra, Derbe and the return journey — Acts 14; 2 Timothy 3:10–11

In Iconium many believed, the city divided, and a plot to stone them made them flee. At Lystra Paul healed a man lame from birth. The crowd shouted that the gods had come down, called Barnabas "Jupiter" and Paul "Mercury" "because he was the chief speaker," and the priest of Jupiter brought oxen to sacrifice. Barnabas and Paul tore their clothes and rushed into the crowd: "We also are men of the same nature as you." Then opponents from Antioch and Iconium arrived, turned the crowd, stoned Paul and dragged him out of the city, supposing he was dead. As the disciples gathered around him he got up and went back into the city. Next day he left for Derbe. Afterwards Paul and Barnabas deliberately went back through Lystra, Iconium and Antioch, strengthening the disciples, telling them "through many afflictions we must enter into God's Kingdom," and appointing elders with prayer and fasting. They returned to Antioch and reported everything God had done and how He had opened a door of faith to the Gentiles. Years later Paul reminded Timothy of what happened at Antioch, Iconium and Lystra.

**Truths:** Refusing honour that belongs to God was urgent. Paul returned to the city where he had been stoned. The mission ended in local leadership and a report to the sending church.

**2026 use:** Follow-up and returning to strengthen what was started; appointing local leaders; reporting back to the sending body. The Jupiter/Mercury confusion may be gently light; the stoning is serious.

## The circumcision dispute and the Jerusalem Council — Acts 15:1–35; Galatians 2:1–10

Men from Judea taught Gentile believers in Antioch that they could not be saved unless circumcised. Paul and Barnabas had "no small discord and discussion with them" and were sent with others to Jerusalem. After much debate Peter spoke about Cornelius; the assembly fell silent while Barnabas and Paul reported the signs and wonders God had done among the Gentiles; James gave judgment. The apostles and elders sent a letter with Judas Barsabbas and Silas, calling Barnabas and Paul "men who have risked their lives for the name of our Lord Jesus Christ." In Galatians Paul describes going up to Jerusalem with Barnabas and Titus, a Greek who was not compelled to be circumcised, laying his gospel privately before the leaders, refusing to yield to "false brothers," and receiving the right hand of fellowship from James, Cephas and John, who asked only that they "remember the poor—which very thing I was also zealous to do."

**Interpretation:** Whether Galatians 2:1–10 describes this council or the earlier famine visit is debated.

**Truths:** A real doctrinal conflict was brought to shared leadership and settled with testimony, Scripture and a written decision carried by trusted people.

**2026 use:** Leadership Council meeting notes, policy decisions, evidence from ministry informing decisions, Silas and Titus relationships, "remember the poor."

## Opposing Peter at Antioch — Galatians 2:11–21

When Peter came to Antioch he ate with Gentile believers until people came from James; then he drew back, "fearing those who were of the circumcision." Other Jewish believers joined him, "so that even Barnabas was carried away with their hypocrisy." Paul wrote, "I resisted him to his face, because he stood condemned," and spoke to Peter "before them all": if Peter, a Jew, lived like a Gentile, how could he compel Gentiles to live like Jews? Paul then set out that no one is justified by works of the law but through faith in Christ, ending, "I have been crucified with Christ, and it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me."

**Silence:** Peter's response and the immediate outcome are not recorded. 2 Peter later calls him "our beloved brother Paul."

**Truths:** Table fellowship was a gospel issue, not etiquette. Paul corrected a pillar publicly because the behaviour was public and others were following it.

**2026 use:** Restricted accountability between senior leaders; the Peter relationship; Barnabas' involvement. Any 2026 disagreement must be a new matter, not an Antioch replay (consistent with Peter's files).

## The sharp disagreement with Barnabas over Mark — Acts 15:36–41

Some time later Paul proposed to Barnabas that they revisit every city where they had preached "to see how they are doing." Barnabas wanted to take John Mark. Paul "didn't think that it was a good idea to take with them someone who had withdrawn from them in Pamphylia, and didn't go with them to do the work." "Then the contention grew so sharp that they separated from each other." Barnabas took Mark and sailed to Cyprus. Paul chose Silas, was commended by the believers to the grace of God, and went through Syria and Cilicia strengthening the churches.

**Silence:** Acts does not say who was right, does not record reconciliation, and never mentions Barnabas again. Later, Paul mentions Barnabas without hostility (1 Corinthians 9:6), writes that the Colossians had received instructions to welcome Mark, "the cousin of Barnabas" (Colossians 4:10), names Mark as a fellow worker (Philemon 24), and asks Timothy to bring Mark "for he is useful to me for service" (2 Timothy 4:11).

**Truths:** Two faithful leaders disagreed about a person's readiness strongly enough to part. The work continued in two teams. The person at the centre of the dispute later became useful to the one who had refused him.

**2026 use:** Mentorship disagreements about readiness; restoration paths; Barnabas and John Mark relationships; Paul's Goal 3. Never resolve by making one side foolish.

## Timothy joins — Acts 16:1–5

At Lystra Paul met Timothy, whose mother was a believing Jewish woman and whose father was Greek, and whom the believers at Lystra and Iconium spoke well of. Paul wanted Timothy to come with him and circumcised him because of the Jews in that area, who knew his father was Greek. They delivered the Jerusalem decisions to the churches, which were strengthened and grew daily.

**Truths:** Paul chose a younger leader with a good local reputation. He made a concession for the sake of mission in Timothy's case, while refusing to compel Titus (Galatians 2:3), because the issue in each case was different.

**2026 use:** Emerging-leader selection, Timothy relationship, contextual decisions that look inconsistent until explained.

## Closed doors, the Macedonian call, and Philippi — Acts 16:6–40; 1 Thessalonians 2:2

The Spirit prevented them from speaking in Asia and would not allow them into Bithynia. At Troas Paul saw a vision of a man of Macedonia pleading, "Come over into Macedonia and help us." (Here the narrative begins to say "we.") At a place of prayer by the river in Philippi they spoke with women gathered there. The Lord opened the heart of Lydia, a seller of purple cloth from Thyatira; she and her household were baptised and she persuaded them to stay at her house. For many days a slave girl with a spirit of divination, who earned her owners money by fortune-telling, followed them shouting. Paul, "greatly annoyed," commanded the spirit to come out of her. Her owners, seeing their profit gone, dragged Paul and Silas before the magistrates. The crowd joined in; they were stripped, beaten with rods, thrown into the inner prison and fastened in stocks. "About midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God, and the prisoners were listening to them." An earthquake opened the doors and loosened everyone's chains. The jailer, about to kill himself, heard Paul shout, "Don't harm yourself, for we are all here!" He asked, "Sirs, what must I do to be saved?" and was told, "Believe in the Lord Jesus Christ, and you will be saved, you and your household." He washed their wounds and was baptised with his household, and fed them at his table. In the morning, when the magistrates sent word to release them quietly, Paul refused: they had been beaten publicly without trial as Roman citizens, so the magistrates must come themselves. The magistrates came and apologised. Paul and Silas went to Lydia's house, encouraged the believers and left. Paul later wrote that they had "suffered before and been shamefully treated" at Philippi.

**Interpretation:** Paul's annoyance at the slave girl is stated; whether it was about her, the spirit or the disruption is not. His insistence on a public release is often read as protecting the young church's standing; the text does not give his reason.

**Truths:** Plans closed before a new door opened. A church began with a businesswoman's household and a jailer's household. Worship happened in pain, before the earthquake. A girl exploited for profit was freed at cost to the team.

**2026 use:** Mission-change reports, hospitality (Lydia), worship under hardship (Silas, Psalmists), Reach-Out, advocacy against exploitation. Beating and imprisonment are never comedy.

## Thessalonica, Berea and Athens — Acts 17; 1 Thessalonians 2:1–3:10

In Thessalonica Paul reasoned from the Scriptures for three Sabbaths that the Christ had to suffer and rise. Some believed, including many devout Greeks and prominent women. Opponents started a riot, attacked Jason's house, and accused the believers of turning the world upside down and proclaiming another king. The believers sent Paul and Silas away by night to Berea, where people examined the Scriptures daily. Opponents followed; Paul was sent to the coast and escorted to Athens, leaving Silas and Timothy behind. In Athens his spirit was provoked by the city full of idols. He reasoned in the synagogue and daily in the marketplace, was called a "babbler" by some philosophers, and was brought to the Areopagus. He began with an altar inscribed "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD," quoted their own poets, and proclaimed the Creator who commands all people to repent and has given assurance of judgment by raising a man from the dead. Some mocked; some wanted to hear more; a few, including Dionysius and a woman named Damaris, believed. In 1 Thessalonians Paul wrote that the team had been gentle "like a nursing mother," had worked night and day so as not to burden anyone, and that when he could bear it no longer he sent Timothy back to strengthen them, and was comforted by Timothy's good report.

**Truths:** Paul adapted his starting point to his audience without changing the message. He worried about new believers he had had to leave suddenly, and sent help.

**2026 use:** Contextual communication, follow-up after abrupt departures, sending a delegate, Timothy and Silas relationships, pastoral affection.

## Corinth: Priscilla and Aquila; "don't be afraid" — Acts 18:1–22; 1 Corinthians 2:1–5

In Corinth Paul found Aquila and Priscilla, Jewish believers recently expelled from Rome under Claudius, stayed with them and worked with them because they shared the trade of tentmaking. When Silas and Timothy arrived from Macedonia, Paul devoted himself to testifying that Jesus was the Christ. After opposition in the synagogue he moved next door to the house of a man named Justus; Crispus, the synagogue ruler, believed with his household. One night the Lord said to Paul in a vision, "Don't be afraid, but speak and don't be silent; for I am with you." He stayed a year and six months. The proconsul Gallio refused to hear charges against him. Paul later wrote that he came to Corinth "in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling," deciding to know nothing among them "except Jesus Christ and him crucified." He sailed with Priscilla and Aquila to Ephesus, left them there, and went on to Caesarea, Jerusalem and Antioch.

**Truths:** Paul was afraid in Corinth, and was told not to be. Ministry and ordinary work shared the same workshop.

**2026 use:** Priscilla and Aquila relationships, work and ministry together, fear in leadership, testimony.

## Ephesus: two years, a riot, and despair — Acts 19; 1 Corinthians 15:32; 16:8–9; 2 Corinthians 1:8–11

In Ephesus Paul met about twelve disciples who knew only John's baptism; they were baptised in the name of Jesus and received the Holy Spirit. After three months in the synagogue he taught daily in the lecture hall of Tyrannus for two years, so that all of Asia heard. God did extraordinary miracles through him. Seven sons of Sceva tried to use "Jesus whom Paul preaches" as a formula; the evil spirit answered, "Jesus I know, and Paul I know, but who are you?" and overpowered them. Many believers confessed their practices, and books of magic worth fifty thousand pieces of silver were burned. Demetrius the silversmith, whose trade in Artemis shrines was threatened, started a riot; the crowd seized Gaius and Aristarchus and shouted "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" for about two hours. Paul wanted to go into the theatre; the disciples, and some officials who were his friends, would not let him. The town clerk dispersed the crowd. Paul wrote from Ephesus of "a great and effective door" and "many adversaries," and later that in Asia they were so utterly burdened beyond their strength that "we despaired even of life."

**Interpretation:** "Fought with animals at Ephesus" (1 Corinthians 15:32) is probably figurative, but the text does not settle it.

**Truths:** Fruitful ministry and serious danger were at their peak together. Paul accepted his team's refusal to let him walk into the theatre.

**2026 use:** Team restraint of a leader, long-term teaching ministry, Aristarchus relationship, honest reporting of despair.

## The Corinthian correspondence: factions, discipline and a letter written with tears — 1 Corinthians 1:10–17; 3:4–9; 4:14–17; 5:1–5; 2 Corinthians 2:1–13; 7:5–16; 10:1–11

The Corinthian church divided into groups: "I follow Paul," "I follow Apollos," "I follow Cephas." Paul asked, "Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you?" and wrote, "I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase." He called himself their father in Christ and sent Timothy to remind them of his ways. He confronted them for tolerating a man living with his father's wife and instructed them to remove him from their fellowship, for the man's ultimate salvation. Later he wrote a painful letter "out of much affliction and anguish of heart… with many tears," chose not to make another painful visit, and urged them to forgive and comfort someone who had been disciplined "lest by any means such a one should be swallowed up with his excessive sorrow." Waiting for news, he could not rest at Troas even though a door was open, because he did not find Titus. In Macedonia "Fightings were outside. Fear was inside." Then Titus arrived with news of their repentance. Paul wrote that he had regretted the letter for a while, but no longer, because their grief led to repentance. Opponents said his letters were "weighty and strong" but his presence weak and his speech despised.

**Interpretation:** Whether the person to be forgiven in 2 Corinthians 2 is the man of 1 Corinthians 5 is debated. The "painful letter" may be lost or may be part of 2 Corinthians; not settled.

**Truths:** Paul refused to let a church rally around him. Discipline aimed at restoration and had to end in forgiveness. Hard letters cost him sleep and peace.

**2026 use:** Restricted pastoral correction and restoration records; faction situations with Apollos and Peter; Titus relationship; Goal 2 (tone). The sexual sin in 1 Corinthians 5 is never comedy.

## Hardships, visions and the thorn — 2 Corinthians 4:7–12; 6:4–10; 11:21–33; 12:1–10

Pressed to defend his ministry against people he called false apostles, Paul "boasted" as a fool: five times forty lashes minus one, three times beaten with rods, once stoned, three times shipwrecked, a night and a day in the open sea, dangers from rivers, robbers, his own people, Gentiles, city, wilderness and sea, false brothers, sleeplessness, hunger, cold and exposure, and "anxiety for all the assemblies." He spoke of a man in Christ caught up to paradise fourteen years earlier (widely understood to be himself). To keep him from becoming conceited, "a thorn in the flesh was given to me: a messenger of Satan to torment me." He begged the Lord three times to remove it and was told, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness." He concluded, "For when I am weak, then am I strong." Elsewhere he wrote that we have this treasure in clay vessels, "pressed on every side, yet not crushed."

**Silence:** The thorn is never identified. Most of the listed beatings and shipwrecks are not narrated in Acts.

**Truths:** Paul's credentials were suffering and weakness, not impressiveness. God did not remove the thorn; He gave grace.

**2026 use:** Testimony, personal development, wellbeing and capacity conversations, Goal 4. No diagnosis of the thorn.

## The collection for the poor in Jerusalem — Galatians 2:10; 1 Corinthians 16:1–4; 2 Corinthians 8–9; Romans 15:25–31; Acts 24:17

Paul organised a collection from Gentile churches for poor believers in Jerusalem. He told the Corinthians to set money aside on the first day of every week so nothing would need collecting when he arrived, and said their approved representatives would carry the gift with letters. He held up the Macedonian churches, who gave beyond their means out of deep poverty. He sent Titus and two respected brothers ahead, one appointed by the churches to travel with the gift, saying, "We are avoiding this, that any man should blame us concerning this abundance which is administered by us," and "Having regard for honorable things, not only in the sight of the Lord, but also in the sight of men." He taught that "God loves a cheerful giver," that giving is measured by what one has, and that the aim is equality. He asked the Romans to pray that the gift would be acceptable to the saints in Jerusalem. Before Felix he said he had come to bring gifts for the needy of his nation.

**Truths:** Generosity was organised, voluntary, proportional and accountable. Transparency protected both the givers and the leaders.

**2026 use:** More Than Enough accountability (the ministry owns the work; Titus leads within it), stewardship reports, giving that crosses cultural lines.

## Troas and Eutychus — Acts 20:5–12

On the first day of the week the believers at Troas gathered to break bread. Paul, planning to leave the next day, talked until midnight in an upper room with many lamps. A young man named Eutychus, sitting in a window, sank into deep sleep "as Paul spoke still longer," fell from the third floor and was taken up dead. Paul went down, threw himself on him, embraced him and said, "Don't be troubled, for his life is in him." He went back up, broke bread, ate and talked until daybreak. The young man was taken home alive, and they were greatly comforted.

**Truths:** The gathering was about teaching and shared bread. A frightening death became comfort.

**2026 use:** Long gatherings, care for young adults, Lifegroup. Paul's long talks may be gently acknowledged; the fall and death are never the punchline.

## Farewell to the Ephesian elders at Miletus — Acts 20:17–38

Hurrying to reach Jerusalem, Paul called the Ephesian elders to Miletus. He reminded them how he had served "with all humility, with many tears," teaching "publicly and from house to house." He said he was going to Jerusalem "bound by the Spirit," not knowing what would happen except that imprisonment and afflictions awaited: "nor do I hold my life dear to myself, so that I may finish my race with joy." He told them they would not see his face again, charged them to guard themselves and the flock, warned that "vicious wolves" would come and that some from among them would distort the truth, and reminded them that he had coveted no one's silver or gold and had worked with his own hands, remembering the words of Jesus: "It is more blessed to give than to receive." He knelt and prayed with them. They wept, embraced him and walked him to the ship.

**Truths:** A leader prepares others for his absence. Integrity with money and tears in ministry belong in the same speech.

**2026 use:** Succession and handover meetings, elder/leader charges, Goal 1, Mentorship.

## Warnings on the way to Jerusalem — Acts 21:1–16

At Tyre the disciples "said to Paul through the Spirit that he should not go up to Jerusalem." They all, with wives and children, walked the team to the beach and prayed. At Caesarea they stayed with Philip the evangelist, one of the Seven, whose four unmarried daughters prophesied. The prophet Agabus took Paul's belt, bound his own feet and hands, and said the Jews at Jerusalem would bind the owner of the belt and hand him to the Gentiles. Paul's companions and the local believers begged him not to go. He answered, "What are you doing, weeping and breaking my heart? For I am ready not only to be bound, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus." When he would not be persuaded they said, "The Lord's will be done."

**Interpretation:** How the Spirit's warnings relate to Paul being "bound by the Spirit" to go is understood differently. The text presents the warnings as true (he was bound) and Paul's decision as faithful.

**Truths:** A team can love a leader, disagree about risk, and still release him to God's will.

**2026 use:** Leadership Council risk discussions, team concern about a leader's decision, Philip the Evangelist relationship. Not a record of Paul being stubborn.

## Arrest in the temple; the speech on the steps — Acts 21:17–22:29

James and the elders welcomed Paul and glorified God for his report, then told him that thousands of Jewish believers had heard he taught Jews to abandon Moses. They asked him to join four men completing a vow and pay their expenses, to show he kept the law. He did. Near the end of the seven days, Jews from Asia who had seen Trophimus the Ephesian with him in the city assumed he had brought Greeks into the temple, stirred up a crowd and began beating him. The Roman commander rescued and chained him. On the barracks steps Paul asked to speak and addressed the crowd "in the Hebrew language" (often understood as Aramaic), telling his story from Gamaliel to Damascus to his commission to the Gentiles. At the word "Gentiles" the crowd shouted that he should not live. The commander ordered him examined by flogging; as they tied him, Paul asked whether it was lawful to flog an uncondemned Roman citizen. The commander, who had bought his own citizenship, was alarmed; Paul said, "But I was born a Roman."

**Truths:** Paul accepted a costly act of conciliation for the sake of Jewish believers, and was falsely accused anyway. He used his legal rights when they protected him from unjust violence.

**2026 use:** Leadership Council decisions made to reduce misunderstanding; false accusations; appropriate use of formal rights.

## Before the council; the plot; "Cheer up, Paul" — Acts 23

Before the Sanhedrin Paul said he had lived before God in good conscience. The high priest Ananias ordered him struck on the mouth. Paul answered, "God will strike you, you whitewashed wall!" When told he had insulted God's high priest, he said, "I didn't know, brothers, that he was high priest," and quoted the command not to speak evil of a ruler of the people. Seeing Pharisees and Sadducees present, he declared that he was on trial for the hope of the resurrection, and the council split. That night "the Lord stood by him and said, 'Cheer up, Paul, for as you have testified about me at Jerusalem, so you must testify also at Rome.'" More than forty men vowed not to eat or drink until they had killed him. Paul's sister's son heard of the ambush and told Paul, who had a centurion take the young man to the commander. The commander sent Paul by night to Governor Felix in Caesarea under heavy guard, with a letter.

**Interpretation:** Why Paul did not recognise the high priest is not explained.

**Truths:** Paul spoke sharply, then submitted to Scripture when corrected. God encouraged him in custody before anything improved. A family member's quiet action saved his life.

**2026 use:** Personal development (Goal 2, sharp speech), testimony, the nephew as a reminder that unnamed people matter.

## Two years under Felix; Festus; the appeal; Agrippa — Acts 24–26

Before Felix, the lawyer Tertullus accused Paul of being a plague and a ringleader of the Nazarene sect. Paul answered point by point and said he had come to bring gifts for his nation. Felix postponed the case, allowed Paul some freedom and let his friends care for him. When Paul spoke with Felix and Drusilla about righteousness, self-control and judgment to come, Felix became afraid and sent him away, though he summoned him often, hoping for a bribe. After two years Felix left him in prison to please the Jewish leaders. His successor Festus proposed a trial in Jerusalem; Paul, knowing the danger, said, "I appeal to Caesar!" When King Agrippa and Bernice visited, Paul told his story again, including his violence against believers and his commission to open people's eyes. Festus shouted, "Paul, you are crazy! Your great learning is driving you insane!" Paul replied that he was speaking sober truth, and asked Agrippa whether he believed the prophets. Agrippa asked, "With a little persuasion are you trying to make me a Christian?" Paul said he prayed that everyone listening would become as he was, "except for these bonds." Agrippa told Festus, "This man might have been set free if he had not appealed to Caesar."

**Truths:** Paul spent years waiting on officials who wanted to avoid a decision. He used every hearing as testimony.

**2026 use:** Restricted movement and long delays; governance and legal interaction; testimony before influential people; gentle humor from Festus' outburst only.

## Voyage, storm and shipwreck — Acts 27

Paul and other prisoners sailed for Italy under a centurion named Julius, who treated Paul kindly. Aristarchus of Thessalonica embarked with them (he is not named again in the voyage account). At Fair Havens, late in the sailing season, Paul warned that the voyage would bring loss of cargo, ship and lives. The centurion followed the pilot and the owner instead. A stormy wind called Euroclydon caught the ship. They undergirded the hull, threw cargo and tackle overboard, and saw neither sun nor stars for many days until "all hope that we would be saved was now taken away." Paul stood up and said they should have listened, then told them to take heart: an angel had told him he must stand before Caesar and that God had granted him everyone sailing with him. "For I believe God, that it will be just as it has been spoken to me." On the fourteenth night, when sailors tried to escape in the lifeboat, Paul told the centurion, "Unless these stay in the ship, you can't be saved," and the soldiers cut the boat loose. At dawn he urged all 276 people to eat, gave thanks to God in front of everyone, broke bread and began eating, and they were encouraged. The ship ran aground and broke up. The soldiers planned to kill the prisoners; the centurion, wanting to save Paul, stopped them. Everyone reached land, swimming or on planks.

**Truths:** Paul's leadership in the crisis was practical as well as spiritual: warning, encouraging, stopping a desertion, getting people to eat. A prisoner became the steadiest person on board. The centurion's respect for Paul saved the other prisoners.

**2026 use:** Crisis-leadership and after-action review, advice not taken, care for exhausted teams, Aristarchus and Luke connections. The danger is serious; the "you should have listened to me" line can carry gentle, very human humor in comments.

## Malta and arrival in Rome — Acts 28

The people of Malta showed "uncommon kindness," lighting a fire in the rain and cold. A viper fastened on Paul's hand as he put sticks on the fire; the islanders decided he was a murderer, then, when nothing happened, that he was a god. Publius, the chief man, hosted them; Paul prayed for his father, who was sick with fever and dysentery, and healed him, and others came and were cured. After three months they sailed on. Believers came from Rome as far as The Market of Appius and The Three Taverns to meet him: "When Paul saw them, he thanked God and took courage." In Rome he was allowed to live by himself with a soldier guarding him. He called the Jewish leaders together, explained his case, and taught them from Moses and the prophets from morning to evening; some believed and some did not. He stayed two whole years in his own rented house, welcoming everyone who came, "with all boldness, without hindrance."

**Silence:** Acts ends there. Paul's trial outcome and death are not recorded.

**Truths:** Paul gathered sticks for the fire like anyone else. Being met on the road by believers gave him courage. Confinement did not end his ministry.

**2026 use:** Hospitality received, encouragement from people who show up, ministry within limits.

## Letters from prison: joy, contentment and co-workers — Philippians 1:12–26; 2:19–30; 4:10–19; Ephesians 3:1; 6:19–22; Colossians 4:2–18; Philemon

From chains (the location is not always stated) Paul wrote that his imprisonment had advanced the gospel and emboldened other believers. Some preached out of rivalry; he rejoiced that Christ was proclaimed anyway. "For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain." He planned to send Timothy, saying, "I have no one else like-minded, who will truly care about you," and sent back Epaphroditus, who had nearly died bringing the Philippians' gift, asking them to honour him. He thanked the Philippians for their partnership and wrote, "I have learned in whatever state I am, to be content in it." He asked for prayer that he would speak boldly, calling himself "an ambassador in chains," and sent Tychicus to tell the churches how he was. He sent Onesimus back to Philemon with a personal appeal: once "useless to you, but now is useful to you and to me," to be received "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother," and "if he has wronged you at all or owes you anything, put that to my account." He greeted co-workers by name, including Aristarchus, "Mark the cousin of Barnabas," Epaphras, "Luke the beloved physician," and Demas.

**Interpretation:** Whether these letters were written from Rome, Caesarea or Ephesus is debated.

**Truths:** Paul's confinement became a hub of relationships: people sent, people received, people commended. He appealed rather than commanded when love could do the work.

**2026 use:** Leadership reports written under constraint, commendation letters, reconciliation (Philemon and Onesimus), honouring support workers (Epaphroditus), Tychicus as communicator.

## Sons in the faith: Timothy and Titus — 1 Corinthians 4:17; Philippians 2:19–22; 2 Corinthians 7:6–7; 8:16–23; 1 Timothy 1:2–3, 18; 4:11–16; 2 Timothy 1:2–8; 2:1–3; Titus 1:4–5; 3:12–13

Paul called Timothy "my beloved and faithful child in the Lord" and Titus "my true child according to a common faith." He sent Timothy to Corinth and Thessalonica to remind and strengthen, left him in Ephesus to stop false teaching, and told him, "Let no man despise your youth," urging him to be an example and to attend to reading, exhortation and teaching. He reminded Timothy of the sincere faith of his grandmother Lois and mother Eunice, told him to stir up the gift given through the laying on of Paul's hands, and wrote, "God didn't give us a spirit of fear, but of power, love, and self-control." He told him to entrust what he had heard "to faithful men who will be able to teach others also." He left Titus in Crete to "set in order the things that were lacking and appoint elders in every city," sent him to Corinth with the collection, and was comforted by his arrival.

**Truths:** Paul delegated real authority to younger leaders in hard places and backed them in writing. His goal was a chain of teachers beyond himself.

**2026 use:** Mentorship Night; delegated assignments; written backing for younger leaders; Timothy and Titus relationships; Goal 1.

## The last letter: deserted, finishing, asking for Mark — 2 Timothy 1:15–18; 4:6–22

Paul wrote that all in Asia had turned away from him, but Onesiphorus searched for him in Rome and was not ashamed of his chain. "For I am already being offered, and the time of my departure has come. I have fought the good fight. I have finished the course. I have kept the faith." Demas had deserted him, "having loved this present world"; Crescens and Titus had gone elsewhere. "Only Luke is with me. Take Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for service." He asked for the cloak he had left at Troas and "the books, especially the parchments." He warned against Alexander the coppersmith. "At my first defense, no one came to help me, but all left me. May it not be held against them. But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me." He asked Timothy to come before winter.

**Uncertainty:** 2 Timothy's authorship is widely disputed. Data Play uses it as Paul's words without arguing the question.

**Truths:** At the end Paul was cold, lonely, still reading, still forgiving, and still asking for co-workers, including the one he had once refused.

**2026 use:** Legacy and succession planning, loyalty and desertion, Luke and John Mark relationships, the cloak-and-parchments request as gentle humor only when the record is not about his death.

## How Peter wrote about Paul — 2 Peter 3:15–16

2 Peter tells readers that "our beloved brother Paul," according to the wisdom given him, wrote about God's patience, and that in all his letters "there are some things that are hard to understand, which the ignorant and unsettled twist."

**Uncertainty:** 2 Peter's authorship is debated (see Peter's profile).

**Truths:** Paul was loved and found difficult by the same colleague. Being hard to understand is not the same as being wrong, but it creates pastoral work.

**2026 use:** Peter relationship; comments on Paul's long reports; teaching clarity.

## Paul's letters as leadership documents

The thirteen letters bearing Paul's name are occasional: written to real churches and people about real problems.

- **Romans** — his fullest statement of the gospel; plans for Jerusalem, Rome and Spain; Phoebe commended; a long list of greetings to co-workers, several of them women (Romans 1:8–15; 9:1–5; 15:14–33; 16:1–23).
- **1 Corinthians** — factions, discipline, lawsuits, marriage, food offered to idols, worship, gifts, love, resurrection, the collection (1:10–17; 5; 13; 15; 16:1–4).
- **2 Corinthians** — the painful visit and letter, comfort in affliction, reconciliation, generosity, defence against false apostles, weakness and the thorn (1:3–11; 2:1–13; 8–9; 10–12).
- **Galatians** — his own story, Antioch, justification by faith, freedom and the Spirit, written with his own large letters (1–2; 6:11).
- **Ephesians** — Jews and Gentiles made one; the church; household and spiritual armour; "an ambassador in chains" (2:11–22; 3:1–13; 6:10–22). Authorship widely debated.
- **Philippians** — joy in prison, humility of Christ, Timothy and Epaphroditus, pressing on, Euodia and Syntyche, contentment (1–4).
- **Colossians** — the supremacy of Christ; co-workers Tychicus, Onesimus, Aristarchus, Mark, Epaphras, Luke, Demas (1:15–20; 4:7–18). Authorship debated.
- **1 and 2 Thessalonians** — affectionate founding memories, persecution, the Lord's return, working quietly; letters from "Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy" (1 Thessalonians 1:1; 2:1–3:10; 2 Thessalonians 1:1; 3:6–17). 2 Thessalonians debated; 2 Thessalonians 2:2 warns against letters falsely claiming to be from the team.
- **1 Timothy and Titus** — ordering churches, qualifications for leaders, false teaching, care for widows (1 Timothy 1; 3; 5; Titus 1–3). Authorship most widely disputed.
- **2 Timothy** — final charge and personal requests (1–4). Authorship widely disputed.
- **Philemon** — a personal appeal for Onesimus (1–25).

**Cross-era notes:** Paul used Moses (2 Corinthians 3:7–18), Elijah (Romans 11:2–4), David (Romans 4:6–8), Isaiah (Romans 10:16; Acts 28:25–27) and Abraham as Scripture. These are Data Play characters. Paul knows them from Scripture only; they have no memory of Paul or of his letters.

**2026 use:** Paul's written feedback style; letters of commendation for leaders moving between ministries; long pastoral correspondence; the handwritten sign-off.

## Paul's death

2 Timothy 4:6–8 records that Paul expected to die soon. Scripture does not describe his death.

**Tradition:** Church tradition says Paul was beheaded in Rome under Nero.

**2026 use:** Rarely. Paul in 2026 is alive and serving. Use only for reflection on finishing faithfully, and never as comedy.
