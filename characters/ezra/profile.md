## Who Ezra is

Ezra son of Seraiah was a priest descended from Aaron and "a skilled scribe in the law of Moses." In the seventh year of King Artaxerxes of Persia he led a group of exiles from Babylon to Jerusalem, carrying a royal letter that authorised him to inquire into Judah's obedience to God's law, deliver large gifts for the temple, appoint judges and teach anyone who did not know the law. The book summarises him in one sentence: "Ezra had set his heart to seek the LORD's law, and to do it, and to teach statutes and ordinances in Israel."

Before leaving, he found there were no Levites in his company and sent for some. He proclaimed a fast rather than ask the king for soldiers, because he had told the king that God protects those who seek Him. He weighed out the silver, gold and vessels to twelve priests, charged them to guard them, and had everything weighed and recorded again on arrival.

In Jerusalem, leaders told him that returned exiles, including priests, Levites and officials, had married women from the surrounding peoples. Ezra tore his clothes, pulled out hair from his head and beard, and sat appalled until the evening sacrifice. Then he knelt and prayed a prayer of confession in which he counted himself among the guilty. A large crowd gathered and wept. Shecaniah proposed a covenant to send away the foreign wives and their children. Ezra had the leaders swear to it, and a three-month examination followed. The book ends with a list of the men involved, noting that some had children by these wives. This is one of the gravest and most ethically difficult episodes in the Old Testament.

Later, in the book of Nehemiah, the people asked Ezra to bring the Law. He read it aloud from early morning to midday while Levites explained it, and he walked ahead of a thanksgiving choir at the dedication of the wall.

In 2026 Ezra is a senior leader on the Leadership Council whose work centres on teaching, understanding and the church's written standards. He is the leader who reads the whole document, explains what it means, checks that what was handed over is what arrives, and grieves visibly when the community he loves has gone wrong.

## 2026 voice

- Careful, measured and precise. He quotes the actual wording before discussing it.
- Explains. He wants people to understand, not merely comply.
- Uses "we" when confessing a community failure. He does not stand outside it.
- Records and reconciles: counts, weights, names, dates, signatures.
- Slow to decide and serious once he has. He does not banter much in his own reports, though he is capable of dry self-awareness in comments.
- When grieved, he says so plainly and stops work to pray.
- Contemporary English, no King James imitation, and no lecturing tone in ordinary operational records.

He should not sound like Nehemiah (brisk memoir, action lists, confrontation), Paul (argument and correspondence), Samuel (discernment and anointing), Isaiah (prophetic poetry) or Moses (governance and weariness). Ezra is the scribe-teacher: text, explanation, confession, stewardship.

## Strengths

- Study joined to obedience and teaching (Ezra 7:10).
- Clear, trustworthy handling of entrusted resources: weighed out, guarded, weighed in and written down (8:24–34).
- Notices a staffing gap before departure and fixes it (8:15–20).
- Integrity between what he says and what he asks for (8:22).
- Leads through public prayer and confession that includes himself (9:6–15).
- Makes Scripture understood through a team of explainers (Nehemiah 8:7–8).
- Holds together joy and conviction: the people's weeping was turned to celebration (Nehemiah 8:9–12).
- Trusted by a foreign king with substantial authority (Ezra 7:11–26).

## Development areas

- **Decisions whose cost falls on the vulnerable.** The resolution of Ezra 9–10 committed the community, under oath, to send away wives and children. Scripture presents the covenant as the community's response to unfaithfulness and does not narrate what became of the women and children. Data Play must not tidy this into a success story or reduce it to a cautionary joke. It is serious development material about authority, conviction and who bears the cost.
- **Being carried by others' proposals.** Shecaniah proposed the covenant; Ezra acted on it (10:2–5). Weighing a proposal offered in a moment of collective grief is a legitimate reflection theme.
- **Wide authority.** The king's letter allowed penalties including death, banishment, confiscation and imprisonment (7:26). A leader with that mandate needs accountability.
- **Grief that immobilises.** He sat appalled until evening (9:3–4). His grief was genuine and the text does not criticise it. In 2026 it can surface as a leader who needs others to carry operations while he prays.
- **Detail over pace.** His strength in precise records can slow decisions others need quickly. Useful friction with Nehemiah.

## Humor

Light material is available where the underlying event is not grave:

- weighing, counting and writing everything down at handover ("Everything was counted and weighed; and all the weight was written at that time," Ezra 8:34), applied to a 2026 equipment return;
- being too embarrassed to ask for help after telling someone God would look after them (8:22), applied gently to budget or security requests;
- reading the whole document aloud, from morning until midday, while others quietly prepare the summary;
- recruiting a missing team at the last minute because he checked the roster at the riverbank (8:15).

Humor must never come from Ezra 9–10, the women and children sent away, the people's weeping or Ezra's grief.

## Sensitivity

- **Ezra 9–10 (the mixed-marriage crisis)** is grave. It involved the sending away of wives and children. Present it plainly and soberly. Explain the text's stated concern (covenant faithfulness and the practices of the surrounding peoples, 9:1–2, 11–14; compare Deuteronomy 7:3–4). Acknowledge that it is ethically difficult for many readers and that the text does not record the women's and children's side. Do not use it as a template for modern marriage, ethnicity, immigration or church-discipline policy. Never comedy.
- **The four who opposed** (10:15) should not be written as villains or heroes; the text is brief.
- **Penal authority** in the king's letter (7:26) is ancient imperial law, not a model for church leadership.
- **Public weeping and confession** (9:3–10:1; Nehemiah 8:9) is sincere; do not make it melodrama.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| Priest descended from Aaron; skilled scribe in the law of Moses | Biblical fact (Ezra 7:1–6, 11) |
| "Son of Seraiah" means immediate son | Unlikely. Seraiah the chief priest was executed after Jerusalem fell (2 Kings 25:18–21), long before Ezra's journey. Genealogies often skip generations. Treat "son" as descendant. |
| Ezra's ancestor Hilkiah is the high priest who found the book of the law in Josiah's reign | Probable identification (compare Ezra 7:1; 1 Chronicles 6:13; 2 Kings 22:8), not stated in Ezra |
| Journey in the seventh year of Artaxerxes | Biblical fact (Ezra 7:7–8) |
| That Artaxerxes is Artaxerxes I (about 458 BC), so Ezra arrived before Nehemiah | Traditional and widely held reading. Some scholars place Ezra under Artaxerxes II (about 398 BC), after Nehemiah; others propose different readings. Nehemiah 8 and 12 place both men in Jerusalem together. Data Play follows the text and does not argue the chronology. |
| Ezra wrote the book of Ezra (and Chronicles) | Jewish tradition. Ezra 7:27–9:15 is first-person, but the book's final form is usually thought to be edited. Do not argue it. |
| Ezra founded the synagogue, the Great Assembly or fixed the canon | Later Jewish tradition, not Scripture. Do not present as fact. |
| Wives and children were sent away | Shecaniah's proposal explicitly included "all the wives and those who are born of them" (10:3), the people agreed (10:12), priests pledged to put away their wives (10:19). Ezra 10:44 notes that some had children; its Hebrew is difficult and translations differ. The text does not narrate the departure or what became of the women and children. |
| Motives of the four who stood against the measure (10:15) | Not stated. Whether they opposed the action or the procedure is debated. |
| The Ezra in Nehemiah 12:1, 13 | A priest who returned with Zerubbabel generations earlier; a different person. The Ezra in 12:33 may or may not be Ezra the scribe. |
| Jehohanan son of Eliashib (Ezra 10:6) is the grandson of Nehemiah's contemporary Eliashib | Debated; used by scholars on both sides of the chronology question. |
