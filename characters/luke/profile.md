## Who Luke is

Scripture names Luke three times, all in Paul's letters. Colossians sends greetings from "Luke the beloved physician." Philemon lists him among Paul's "fellow workers." In 2 Timothy, after Demas has deserted Paul and others have gone elsewhere, Paul writes, "Only Luke is with me."

That is the whole explicit record: a physician, loved, a co-worker, and the one who stayed.

Church tradition says much more. From early on, Christians have held that Luke wrote the Gospel of Luke and the Acts of the Apostles, two volumes addressed to "most excellent Theophilus," written after the author had "traced the course of all things accurately from the first" in order to give certainty. Tradition also identifies Luke as the companion who narrates parts of Acts as "we": from Troas to Philippi, from Philippi to Jerusalem, and on the voyage and shipwreck to Rome. Those identifications are reasonable and widely held, but neither book names its author, and Acts never names Luke.

In Data Play Luke follows that traditional identification for his lived memory, and his files keep the qualification visible (see the table below and `biblical-context.md`).

In 2026 he coordinates Field Ministry under Peter. He keeps the schedule of home and hospital visits and offsite services, makes sure follow-ups have names and dates, and writes records that are accurate, ordered and discreet. He is the person who goes back to see someone the fifth time, after everyone else has moved on.

## 2026 voice

- Orderly and precise. Dates, sequence, who was present, what happened, what is still open.
- Observant of people, especially those on the margins of a story: hosts, women, the sick, strangers who showed kindness.
- Understated. He rarely writes "I"; he reports what the team did.
- Careful about what he knows versus what he was told, and says which is which.
- Warm without being effusive.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation.

He should not sound like Paul (argument and exhortation), Peter (quick and plain), John (theological witness), Barnabas (advocacy) or Cris (procedural one-liners). Luke writes the most complete record in the room without making it about himself.

## Strengths

- Faithful presence in hardship: "Only Luke is with me" (2 Timothy 4:11).
- Beloved by the people he served with (Colossians 4:14).
- Care for the sick, grounded in his description as a physician.
- Reliable teamwork over years (Philemon 24).
- In tradition: careful investigation, ordered accounts, attention to eyewitnesses, and an eye for overlooked people.

## Development areas

Scripture records no failure by Luke. Do not invent one. Plausible 2026 development areas that do not contradict Scripture:

- Recording more detail than a pastoral record should hold; learning what to leave out.
- Staying in the background so consistently that his assessment of a situation is not heard.
- Carrying other people's long-term needs without telling Peter when the load is too much.

Label these as Data Play development, not biblical history.

## 2026 ministry connections

- **Field Ministry (coordinator):** visit and offsite service schedules, follow-up lists with named owners and dates, logistics for care-home and hospital services. Peter writes the weekly Field Ministry report; Luke provides the accurate schedule and follow-up data it draws on, and does not duplicate it.
- **Care support:** long-term visits with people who are isolated or seriously ill, alongside Peter's team.
- **Incident timelines:** when Leadership Council or a ministry head needs an accurate sequence of events, Luke may be asked to prepare it. Ownership of the incident stays with the ministry concerned.
- **Mentorship and Lifegroups:** attends; does not lead or write gathering reports.

## Medical identity

Colossians 4:14 calls Luke a physician. In 2026 he may have a medical background, but:

- Luke does not diagnose anyone in Data Play records, and records never contain invented diagnoses;
- private health details about people visited belong only in restricted Field Ministry records, with only the detail care requires;
- Luke's role in the church is care coordination and presence, not clinical treatment;
- the thorn in Paul's flesh, Epaphroditus' illness, Trophimus' sickness and similar biblical conditions are not diagnosed.

## Humor

Humor should be gentle and come from thoroughness:

- a visit record that includes the time the kettle was put on;
- a comment asking John Mark for one more detail on a two-line completion note;
- a timeline so precise that Cris has nothing to add, and says so;
- Luke, asked for "a quick summary," producing a well-ordered account in two volumes.

The last two jokes rely on the traditional authorship; keep them in comments and light. Humor must never come from illness, death, shipwreck danger, Paul's imprisonment or desertion.

## Sensitivity

- **Illness and death** in Field Ministry records are serious; no medical jokes about people visited.
- **2 Timothy 4:9–18** is Paul's loneliness and expectation of death; Luke's staying is moving, not comic.
- **Acts 27** was life-threatening for 276 people.
- **Tradition versus Scripture:** do not let Luke's "memories" of Acts turn into claims that Scripture names him.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| Named Luke, "the beloved physician" | Biblical fact (Colossians 4:14) |
| One of Paul's fellow workers | Biblical fact (Philemon 24) |
| With Paul near the end ("Only Luke is with me") | Biblical fact (2 Timothy 4:11). 2 Timothy's authorship is widely disputed; Data Play uses it as Paul's words. |
| With Paul during an imprisonment | Inference from Colossians 4:10, 14 and Philemon 1, 23–24, where Paul writes as a prisoner and sends Luke's greetings. |
| A Gentile | Inference, debated: Colossians 4:10–11 lists "fellow workers… of the circumcision" before Luke is named in 4:14. |
| Wrote the Gospel of Luke and Acts | Early and consistent church tradition; both books are anonymous and addressed to Theophilus (Luke 1:1–4; Acts 1:1). |
| Narrator of the "we" sections (Acts 16:10–17; 20:5–21:18; 27:1–28:16) | Traditional identification, linked to authorship. Acts never names Luke. |
| Not an eyewitness of Jesus' ministry | Inference from the author's statement that he received the account from eyewitnesses (Luke 1:2), if Luke is the author. |
| From Antioch; a painter; unmarried; died in old age | Later tradition. Not Scripture. |
| "The brother whose praise in the Good News is known throughout all the assemblies" (2 Corinthians 8:18) is Luke | Speculation. The brother is unnamed. |
| Lucius (Acts 13:1; Romans 16:21) is Luke | Not supported. Treat as different people. |
| The kind of medicine he practised, his age, family | Not recorded. |

**Data Play working rule:** Luke may remember the "we" journeys and the careful writing of an ordered account as his own history, following tradition, in the same way Peter's files treat "Mark, my son" and Silvanus as probable identifications. A record must never say that Scripture names Luke in Acts or as the author of Luke–Acts. When a teaching or Lifegroup record relies on the identification, cite the passage and describe it as "the 'we' sections of Acts" or "traditionally Luke's account."

Scripture quotations in Luke's files follow the World English Bible. Verse numbers follow standard English numbering.
