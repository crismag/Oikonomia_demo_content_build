# Jeremiah — Biblical Context Library

This file records Jeremiah's lived biblical history that may surface in 2026 Oikonomia content. These are not 2026 reenactments. They are Jeremiah's memories, laments, conflicts, losses and leadership formation.

When an account is used in a record, explain enough that a reader who does not know the Bible can follow who was there, what happened and why it mattered.

Quotations follow the World English Bible (WEB). Where a statement is interpretation or tradition rather than explicit text, it is marked.

**Order note:** The book of Jeremiah is not arranged chronologically, and its Greek version is shorter and differently ordered. The sections below follow the kings named in the text where possible.

---

## The setting — Jeremiah 1:1–3; 2 Kings 22–25

Jeremiah's ministry ran from the thirteenth year of Josiah, a reforming king, through Jehoiakim and Zedekiah to the destruction of Jerusalem by Babylon and beyond. Josiah died in battle; his successors were caught between Egypt and Babylon. Babylon deported leaders and craftsmen in stages and finally burned Jerusalem and the temple.

**2026 use:** Background for every Jeremiah record. Explain briefly.

## The call: "I am a child" — Jeremiah 1

God said, "Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you. Before you were born, I sanctified you. I have appointed you a prophet to the nations." Jeremiah said, "Ah, Lord GOD! Behold, I don't know how to speak; for I am a child." God answered, "Don't say, 'I am a child;' for you must go to whomever I send you, and you must say whatever I command you. Don't be afraid because of them, for I am with you to rescue you." God touched his mouth: "Behold, I have put my words in your mouth." His commission was "to uproot and to tear down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant."

God showed him an almond branch ("I watch over my word to perform it") and a boiling pot tipping from the north. He told Jeremiah to get ready: "I have made you today a fortified city, an iron pillar, and bronze walls … They will fight against you, but they will not prevail against you; for I am with you."

**Fact and boundary:** His exact age is not recorded.

**Truths:** God's call did not wait for confidence. Four of the six verbs in the commission are about tearing down; two are about building and planting. God promised presence and rescue, not an absence of opposition.

**2026 use:** Testimony, Mentorship for young or insecure leaders, goals, preparing for hard assignments.

## The temple sermon — Jeremiah 7:1–15; 26:1–24

At the temple gate Jeremiah told worshippers, "Don't trust in lying words, saying, 'The LORD's temple, the LORD's temple, the LORD's temple, are these.'" Amend your ways, execute justice, don't oppress the foreigner, fatherless and widow, don't shed innocent blood or follow other gods. "Has this house, which is called by my name, become a den of robbers in your eyes?" God would make the temple like Shiloh, the earlier sanctuary that had been destroyed.

Chapter 26 records the reaction. Priests, prophets and people seized him: "You shall surely die!" Officials came to hear the case. Jeremiah said, "as for me, behold, I am in your hand," but warned that killing him would bring innocent blood on the city. The officials and people said he did not deserve death. Elders recalled that Hezekiah had not killed the prophet Micah for a similar warning but had sought the LORD. Another prophet, Uriah son of Shemaiah, preached the same message; King Jehoiakim hunted him down in Egypt and killed him. "But the hand of Ahikam the son of Shaphan was with Jeremiah," so he was not handed over to be killed.

**Truths:** Religious activity can become a false security that excuses injustice. The same message cost one prophet his life and another nearly his. Jeremiah survived partly because an official stood with him.

**2026 use:** Teaching on worship and justice, restricted leadership reports on false security, testimony, advocacy. Uriah's death is grave.

## Plots in Anathoth; footmen and horses — Jeremiah 11:18–12:6

God showed Jeremiah that men of his hometown were plotting against him: "Let's destroy the tree with its fruit." He had been "like a gentle lamb that is led to the slaughter." They had told him not to prophesy in the LORD's name or they would kill him. Jeremiah asked God, "Why does the way of the wicked prosper?" God answered with a harder question: "If you have run with the footmen, and they have wearied you, then how can you contend with horses?" and warned that his own brothers and his father's house had dealt treacherously with him.

**Truths:** Opposition from home cuts deepest. God did not answer "why" directly; He prepared Jeremiah for more.

**2026 use:** Restricted wellbeing, perseverance, Mentorship, Lifegroup on unanswered questions. Serious.

## Complaints and God's correction — Jeremiah 15:10–21

"Woe is me, my mother, that you have borne me, a man of strife." Jeremiah told God, "Your words were found, and I ate them. Your words were to me a joy and the rejoicing of my heart … I sat alone because of your hand." Then: "Why is my pain perpetual, and my wound incurable … Will you indeed be to me as a deceitful brook, like waters that fail?" God answered: "If you return, then I will bring you again, that you may stand before me; and if you take out the precious from the vile, you will be as my mouth. They will return to you, but you will not return to them." He repeated the promise of a bronze wall.

**Truths:** God let Jeremiah say the worst thing he felt, then called him back from it. Joy in God's word and deep pain lived in the same man.

**2026 use:** Personal development, restricted reflection, Mentorship about the line between honest lament and accusation.

## No wife, no funerals, no feasts — Jeremiah 16:1–9

God told Jeremiah not to marry or have children "in this place," not to go into houses of mourning, and not to go into houses of feasting, as signs of the coming loss of ordinary life: "the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride."

**Truths:** Jeremiah's own life, including loneliness, carried the message.

**2026 use:** Serious reflection on singleness and cost in ministry. Do not invent a romantic history or a family.

## Sign-acts: the belt, the potter, the broken jar — Jeremiah 13:1–11; 18:1–12; 19:1–13

God told Jeremiah to buy a linen belt, wear it, hide it in a rock crevice by the Euphrates, and retrieve it later; it was ruined, "profitable for nothing," like the pride of Judah. At the potter's house he watched a spoiled pot reworked into another, and heard that God may relent if a nation turns, or withhold good if it turns to evil. Later he bought a clay jar, took elders and priests to the Valley of Hinnom where children had been burned as offerings to Baal, and smashed it: so God would break the city beyond repair.

**Interpretation:** Whether Jeremiah travelled to the Euphrates itself or to a nearer place with a similar name is discussed.

**Truths:** God's word was shown, not only spoken. The potter image holds both warning and the possibility of change.

**2026 use:** Teaching, light procurement humor about the belt and jar (never about Hinnom or its victims).

## The stocks and the fire shut up in his bones — Jeremiah 20

Pashhur son of Immer, the priest in charge of the temple, struck Jeremiah and put him in the stocks at the upper Benjamin Gate. Released the next day, Jeremiah gave Pashhur a new name, Magor-missabib ("terror on every side"), and announced his exile. Then Jeremiah prayed: "LORD, you have persuaded me, and I was persuaded … I have become a laughingstock all day." He said that if he tried not to speak, "then there is in my heart as it were a burning fire shut up in my bones. I am weary with holding it in. I can't." He praised God for delivering the needy, and then cursed the day he was born: "Why did I come out of the womb to see labor and sorrow, that my days should be consumed with shame?"

**Boundary:** Scripture records anguish and a curse on his birthday. Do not attach a clinical label. The name meaning is a translation of the Hebrew.

**Truths:** Praise and despair sit side by side in the same chapter without resolution. Jeremiah could not stop speaking even when he wanted to.

**2026 use:** Restricted wellbeing and lament, testimony, Mentorship. Never comedy.

## False prophets and Hananiah — Jeremiah 23:16–17; 27–28

God told Jeremiah to wear a yoke of straps and bars as a sign that the nations must serve Babylon. In the temple the prophet Hananiah announced that within two years God would break Babylon's yoke and bring back the exiles and temple vessels. Jeremiah said, "Amen! May the LORD do so," but reminded him that a prophet who predicts peace is known to be sent by God only when it happens. Hananiah took the yoke off Jeremiah's neck and broke it. "Then the prophet Jeremiah went his way." Later God sent Jeremiah back: Hananiah had broken wooden bars and made iron ones in their place. "The LORD has not sent you, but you make this people trust in a lie." Hananiah died that year.

**Truths:** Jeremiah wished the good news were true. He did not win the public argument on the day; he walked away and waited for God's word. Comforting messages are not true because they comfort.

**2026 use:** Discernment in leadership meetings, restricted reports on competing messages, Mentorship. Hananiah's death is not a triumph line.

## Letter to the exiles — Jeremiah 29

After the first deportation, Jeremiah sent a letter to the exiles in Babylon: "Build houses and dwell in them. Plant gardens and eat their fruit. Take wives and father sons and daughters … Seek the peace of the city where I have caused you to be carried away captive, and pray to the LORD for it; for in its peace you will have peace." He warned them not to listen to prophets promising a quick return. "After seventy years are accomplished for Babylon, I will visit you … For I know the thoughts that I think toward you," says the LORD, "thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you hope and a future." Shemaiah, a prophet among the exiles, wrote to the priests in Jerusalem asking why they had not restrained Jeremiah in the stocks for sending such a letter (29:24–28).

**Context:** 29:11 is addressed to the exiled community, after telling them their stay would be long.

**Truths:** Faithfulness sometimes means settling in and serving where you did not choose to be, for longer than you want. Hope is long-horizon, not a quick fix.

**2026 use:** Teaching, Lifegroup, long-term goals, pastoral care for people in seasons they did not choose. Always keep 29:11 in context.

## The Rechabites — Jeremiah 35

God sent Jeremiah to bring the Rechabite family into a temple room and offer them wine. They refused because their ancestor Jonadab had commanded them not to drink wine or build houses. God contrasted their faithfulness to a human ancestor's command with Judah's refusal to listen to God.

**2026 use:** Teaching on faithfulness; not a joke about abstinence.

## The scroll burned and rewritten — Jeremiah 36; 45

In Jehoiakim's fourth year God told Jeremiah to write all His words on a scroll. Jeremiah dictated to Baruch son of Neriah. Because Jeremiah was "restricted" from the temple, he sent Baruch to read it publicly on a fast day. Micaiah son of Gemariah heard it and told the officials, who had Baruch read it to them. They were afraid, asked how it was written ("He dictated all these words to me with his mouth, and I wrote them with ink in the book"), told Baruch and Jeremiah to hide, and reported to the king. The king sat by a fire in his winter house. As Jehudi read three or four columns at a time, the king cut them off with a knife and threw them into the fire until the whole scroll was burned. Three officials urged him not to. The king ordered the arrest of Baruch and Jeremiah, "but the LORD hid them." God told Jeremiah to write it all again. Baruch wrote the new scroll from Jeremiah's dictation, "and many similar words were added to them."

In that same period Baruch said, "Woe is me now! For the LORD has added sorrow to my pain! I am weary with my groaning." God, through Jeremiah, told him not to seek great things for himself, but promised him his life wherever he went (Jeremiah 45).

**Truths:** A hostile leader can destroy a document; he cannot destroy the word. The work was redone, not abandoned. Jeremiah's helper was weary too, and God spoke to him personally.

**2026 use:** Documents and preservation, hostile review of a report, redoing work after loss, caring for assistants and volunteers. The burning is serious; version-control humor only around the rewrite.

## Arrest, cistern and Ebed-melech — Jeremiah 37–38

When Babylon's army briefly withdrew, Jeremiah set out for Benjamin to receive a share of family property. At the Benjamin Gate a guard captain, Irijah, accused him of deserting to the Babylonians. "That is false!" The officials beat him and imprisoned him in a dungeon in Jonathan the scribe's house for many days. King Zedekiah brought him out secretly and asked, "Is there any word from the LORD?" "There is. … You will be delivered into the hand of the king of Babylon." Jeremiah asked not to be sent back to die in that dungeon; Zedekiah moved him to the court of the guard and gave him a daily loaf of bread until the city's bread ran out.

Officials heard him telling people that those who surrendered to Babylon would live. They told the king he was weakening the soldiers and should die. Zedekiah said, "Behold, he is in your hand; for the king can't do anything to oppose you." They lowered Jeremiah by ropes into a cistern with no water, only mud, "and Jeremiah sank in the mire."

Ebed-melech, an Ethiopian official in the palace, went to the king: "these men have done evil in all that they have done to Jeremiah the prophet … He is likely to die in the place where he is, because of the famine." The king told him to take thirty men. Ebed-melech brought old rags and worn-out clothes, lowered them down, and told Jeremiah to put them under his armpits under the ropes. They pulled him out. Jeremiah stayed in the court of the guard.

Zedekiah met him secretly again. Jeremiah said, "If I declare it to you, will you not surely put me to death? If I give you counsel, you will not listen to me." The king swore to protect him. Jeremiah urged him to surrender to save his life and the city. Zedekiah said he was afraid of Judeans who had already defected. He told Jeremiah to keep the conversation secret and to tell the officials only that he had asked not to be sent back to Jonathan's house. Jeremiah did so.

**Truths:** A weak leader handed a prophet over to people he feared. A foreign palace official spoke up, and thought about the ropes cutting under the arms. Kindness was practical.

**2026 use:** Advocacy, courage, restricted reports on mistreatment, the value of practical care in rescue. Serious. The rags can be honoured as care; they are not a joke.

## Buying the field at Anathoth — Jeremiah 32

In Zedekiah's tenth year, with Babylon besieging Jerusalem and Jeremiah confined in the court of the guard, God told him his cousin Hanamel would come and ask him to buy the family field in Anathoth, since he had the right of redemption. Hanamel came. Jeremiah bought it for seventeen shekels of silver, signed and sealed the deed, called witnesses, weighed the money, and gave both the sealed and the open copies to Baruch in front of everyone, telling him to put them in a clay jar "that they may last many days. For the LORD of Armies, the God of Israel says: 'Houses and fields and vineyards will yet again be bought in this land.'" Then Jeremiah prayed, "Ah Lord GOD! … There is nothing too hard for you," and still admitted his confusion: God had told him to buy a field while the city was being handed to the Babylonians. God answered, "Behold, I am the LORD, the God of all flesh. Is there anything too hard for me?" and promised that fields would again be bought in the land.

**Truths:** Hope was a legal transaction with witnesses in a city about to fall. Jeremiah obeyed first and voiced his confusion after. Careful documentation served a future he would not see.

**2026 use:** Long-term goals, testimony, planning in hard seasons, documentation practices, Lifegroup on hope.

## The new covenant — Jeremiah 31:31–34; 32:36–41

"Behold, the days come," says the LORD, "that I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah … I will put my law in their inward parts, and I will write it in their heart. I will be their God, and they shall be my people … for I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more."

**Interpretation:** Hebrews 8:8–13 applies this to the covenant through Jesus. Jeremiah has no memory of Hebrews; in 2026 he can teach how the church reads it.

**2026 use:** Teaching, Communion reflections, hope. Handle with reverence.

## The fall of Jerusalem — Jeremiah 39; 52; 40:1–6

In Zedekiah's eleventh year the city wall was breached. Zedekiah fled by night and was captured near Jericho. At Riblah the king of Babylon killed Zedekiah's sons before his eyes, killed the nobles of Judah, put out Zedekiah's eyes and took him in chains to Babylon. The Babylonians burned the palace and houses and broke down the walls. Most of the remaining people were deported; some of the poorest were left and given fields.

Nebuchadnezzar ordered that Jeremiah be looked after. He was taken from the court of the guard and entrusted to Gedaliah son of Ahikam. Jeremiah 40 adds that he was later found in chains among the captives at Ramah, and the Babylonian commander Nebuzaradan released him and let him choose to go to Babylon or stay. He went to Gedaliah at Mizpah. God also sent Jeremiah to promise Ebed-melech that he would be kept safe because he had trusted God.

**Note:** Chapters 39 and 40 give two accounts of Jeremiah's release; readers harmonise them in different ways.

**Truths:** What Jeremiah had warned about for decades happened. Being proved right brought no pleasure. God remembered the man who had rescued him.

**2026 use:** Grief and lament, restricted reflection, testimony. Never comedy.

## Gedaliah's murder and the flight to Egypt — Jeremiah 40:7–44:30

Gedaliah governed the survivors at Mizpah until Ishmael son of Nethaniah murdered him at a meal, with others. The survivors, led by Johanan son of Kareah, feared Babylonian reprisal. They came to Jeremiah: "pray for us to the LORD your God … Whether it is good, or whether it is bad, we will obey." Jeremiah promised to keep nothing back. After ten days God's word came: stay in the land and God would build them up; go to Egypt and the sword and famine they feared would follow them there. Johanan and the "proud men" said, "You speak falsely," and blamed Baruch for turning him against them. They took everyone to Egypt, including "Jeremiah the prophet, and Baruch the son of Neriah." In Egypt Jeremiah continued to speak, and the people refused, saying life had been better when they burned incense to "the queen of the sky."

**Truths:** People asked for guidance and rejected it the moment it disagreed with a decision they had already made. Jeremiah went with them anyway and kept speaking.

**Silence:** Jeremiah's death is not recorded. The tradition that he was stoned in Egypt is not Scripture.

**2026 use:** Leadership meetings where a decision has been made before consultation, restricted reports, perseverance goals, Lifegroup. Gedaliah's murder is grave.

## Later references to Jeremiah (Jeremiah has no memory of these)

- **2 Chronicles 35:25** — Jeremiah lamented Josiah (within his lifetime; he remembers this).
- **2 Chronicles 36:12, 21–22; Ezra 1:1** — Zedekiah's refusal to humble himself before Jeremiah (within his lifetime); the seventy years and Cyrus's decree as fulfilment of his word (after his lifetime).
- **Daniel 9:2** — Daniel understood the seventy years from Jeremiah's writings.
- **Matthew 2:17–18** — Jeremiah 31:15 (Rachel weeping) cited at the killing of Bethlehem's children.
- **Matthew 16:14** — some thought Jesus was Jeremiah.
- **Hebrews 8:8–13** — the new covenant.
- **Lamentations** — traditionally associated with Jeremiah; the book is anonymous.
