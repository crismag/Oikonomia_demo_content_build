## Who Peter is

Simon, called Peter (Cephas), was a Galilean fisherman working with his brother Andrew and in partnership with James and John. He left his nets to follow Jesus. He became the most visible of the Twelve: often the first to answer, one of the three closest to Jesus, the disciple who denied Him three times, the one Jesus restored and told to feed His sheep, and the leading public voice of the early Jerusalem church.

His biblical story is not only impulsiveness. It is a man of real insight and real misunderstanding, real courage and real fear, who was loved through a public failure and then entrusted with shepherding other people. Over time Peter moves from swinging a sword to telling leaders not to lord it over the people in their care.

In 2026 he is a pastoral leader. He carries Lifegroups, visits people in hospital and at home through Field Ministry, sits on the Leadership Council and is trusted with people at their lowest. He still moves quickly. What has changed is what he does with that speed.

## 2026 voice

- Plainspoken, warm and physical. He talks about boats, nets, fire, fish, doors and roads before he talks about frameworks.
- Short sentences. He says what he thinks, and says so first.
- Names his own failures without drama and without hiding. He does not need anyone to pretend the denial did not happen.
- Asks "What do we do now?" and means it.
- Quick to volunteer, quick to promise, and learning to leave room before answering.
- Not a management consultant and not a theologian by style, although he can explain Scripture clearly when he has to.
- Contemporary English. No King James imitation.

He should not sound like Paul (argument, correspondence, doctrine), John (careful witness and detail), Moses (governance and weariness) or David (emotional poetry).

## Strengths

- Willingness to step out first when others are still watching.
- Genuine confession of who Jesus is, and loyalty when others walk away.
- Pastoral warmth. People bring him their sick, their dead and their questions.
- Courage under official pressure after Pentecost.
- Ability to explain a controversial decision to critics step by step.
- Teachable: he changed his mind about Gentiles when God showed him, and accepted public correction.
- Trusted by other leaders as one of the "pillars".
- Knows from experience how to restore someone after failure.

## Development areas

- Speaking before he understands, as at the Transfiguration and when he rebuked Jesus.
- Acting with force when the moment calls for restraint (the sword in Gethsemane).
- Overconfident promises about his own faithfulness.
- Fear of people's opinion: the denials, and withdrawing from Gentile believers at Antioch when certain people arrived.
- Comparing his assignment with someone else's ("Lord, what about this man?").
- Being the person everyone looks to without letting people form a faction around him.

Failure is not his identity. His restoration is not a way of pretending the failures were small.

## Core relationships

See `relationships.md`. The most important:

- **Andrew** — his brother, who first brought him to Jesus.

## 2026 ministry connections

- **Lifegroups:** leads a weekday group. His Lifegroup reports carry how the group went, members' needs, testimony and follow-up.
- **Field Ministry (head):** visitation, hospital and home care, offsite gatherings. This matches his biblical pattern of travelling to believers at Lydda and Joppa (Acts 9:32–43).
- **Leadership Council:** a senior voice, especially on pastoral care, controversial decisions and cross-cultural inclusion.
- **Mentorship Night:** restoring leaders after failure ("strengthen your brothers").
- **Greeters and Ushers / Reach-Out:** newcomer and cross-cultural welcome, drawn from the Cornelius account. Operational greeting belongs to Greeters and Ushers, not to Peter.
- **Victuals / More Than Enough:** only by connection, e.g. Tabitha's care for widows or "remember the poor". Food and distribution work belongs to those ministries.

## Humor

Light material is available where the underlying event is not grave:

- over-volunteering and saying "yes" before checking (the temple-tax question, Matthew 17:24–27);
- promising to do three things when one was asked;
- building plans before the meeting has finished (the three tents, when he "didn't know what to say");
- administrative review of how quickly he speaks up in meetings;
- Rhoda leaving him knocking at the gate for joy (Acts 12:13–16). This can be gentle only if it never trivialises James' death or Herod's persecution earlier in the same chapter.

Humor must not come from the denial, his grief, Ananias and Sapphira, James' death, beatings or imprisonment. Peter is never written as foolish.

## Sensitivity

- **The denial** (Matthew 26:69–75; Luke 22:54–62; John 18:15–27) is serious failure and grief, followed by restoration (John 21). Never comedy.
- **Malchus** (John 18:10–11; Luke 22:50–51) can support a restraint or incident review. The violence is not the joke, and Jesus' healing of the servant matters.
- **Ananias and Sapphira** (Acts 5:1–11) involve two deaths. Handle soberly; never use them for administrative humor about giving.
- **Persecution** (Acts 4–5; 12) involves threats, flogging, imprisonment and James' execution.

## Fact, inference and tradition

| Matter | Status |
|---|---|
| Fisherman from Bethsaida with a house in Capernaum, brother of Andrew | Biblical fact (John 1:44; Mark 1:16, 29) |
| Married; a mother-in-law healed by Jesus; a believing wife who travelled | Biblical fact (Mark 1:29–31; 1 Corinthians 9:5). Her name and any children are unknown; do not invent them. |
| Father's name | Given as Jonah in John 1:42 and 21:15 (WEB); some manuscripts and translations read "John". Avoid building on it. |
| "The disciple whom Jesus loved" is John son of Zebedee | Traditional identification, not stated in the text. Scenes with Peter and the beloved disciple should name "the disciple whom Jesus loved" or qualify the identification. |
| Meaning of "on this rock" (Matthew 16:18) | Interpreted differently across Christian traditions. Do not settle it in Data Play. |
| 1 Peter and 2 Peter written by Peter | Both letters name Peter as author. 2 Peter's authorship is widely debated by scholars. Data Play may use both as Peter's words, but should not argue the question. |
| "Babylon" in 1 Peter 5:13 means Rome | Common interpretation, not explicit. |
| "Mark, my son" (1 Peter 5:13) is John Mark | Probable identification, not explicit. |
| Silvanus (1 Peter 5:12) is Paul's companion Silas | Probable identification. |
| Peter's death by crucifixion in Rome | Church tradition. Scripture indicates only that his death would glorify God and that he would be led where he did not want to go (John 21:18–19; 2 Peter 1:14). |
| Mark's Gospel draws on Peter's preaching | Early church tradition, not Scripture. |
